<Box kind="care" title="Call emergency services">

**Call emergency services** for collapse during the race, confusion or odd behaviour, a seizure, chest pain, or a runner who isn't breathing normally. If someone isn't breathing normally, start CPR and send for a defibrillator (AED). Among runners in US marathons and half marathons, bystander CPR was the strongest predictor of survival after a cardiac arrest<Cite id="I16" />.

**Seek care now** for muscle pain or weakness far beyond what the race should cause, marked swelling of a muscle, or tea- or cola-coloured urine<Cite id="S21" />. See <TopicLink to="injuries-health/red-flags">red flags</TopicLink>.

</Box>

## Keep walking

When you stop running suddenly, blood can pool in your legs and your blood pressure drops for a moment. Collapse just after the finish line is now thought to come mainly from this, rather than from dehydration or overheating. Collapse during the race, before the finish, is more likely to have a serious cause<Cite id="C6" />.

So keep walking for a few minutes after you cross the line. If you feel faint, lie down with your legs raised and tell a first-aider. Medical teams at the finish will check anyone who collapses, because the signs of heat stroke, low blood sodium and heart problems can look similar at first.

## Warm, fed and watered

- **Get warm.** You cool down fast once you stop, especially if you're wet. Put on dry clothes.
- **Eat** something with carbohydrate and protein once you feel ready. See <TopicLink to="fuel-recovery/before-during">before and during runs</TopicLink>.
- **Drink to thirst**, as during the race. There's no need to drink lots if you're not thirsty. See <TopicLink to="fuel-recovery/fluids-sodium">fluids and sodium</TopicLink>.

## Soreness

Delayed muscle soreness usually starts within a day of unaccustomed exercise, peaks around one to three days after, and fades within about a week<Cite id="I3" />. After a long race, stairs and downhills may be hard for a few days.

Soreness that is spread out over your muscles is expected. Pain in one spot, pain in a joint or bone, or pain that gets worse is different. See <TopicLink to="injuries-health/soreness-or-pain">soreness or pain?</TopicLink>

## Back to running

Rest or walk for the first few days, then start with short, easy runs. A common rule of thumb after a marathon is to keep running easy for a couple of weeks before any hard sessions. It isn't based on trials, so go by how your legs feel. See <TopicLink to="fuel-recovery/rest-days">rest days</TopicLink> and <TopicLink to="training/building-up">building up gradually</TopicLink>.
