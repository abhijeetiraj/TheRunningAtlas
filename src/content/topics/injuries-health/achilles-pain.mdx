<Box kind="care" title="Possible rupture: seek care now">

**Seek care now** if you feel or hear a snap or pop at the back of the ankle, have sudden severe pain there or in the calf, can't carry on, or can't rise up on the toes of that leg. These are signs of an Achilles tendon rupture, which is often mistaken for a sprain<Cite id="I9" />. Don't walk it off.

</Box>

## What it is

The Achilles tendon joins the calf muscles to the heel bone. It's the biggest spring in your leg, storing and returning energy with every step. See <TopicLink to="how-running-works/springs-and-tendons">springs and tendons</TopicLink>.

Achilles tendinopathy is pain and stiffness in the tendon, usually from more load than it's ready for. Pooling studies, it affected about 9 to 11% of runners during follow-up, and it was among the most common injuries in ultramarathon runners<Cite id="I1" />.

It can be felt in the middle of the tendon, a few centimetres above the heel, or where the tendon joins the heel bone. Physiotherapists may adjust the exercises for each, so it's worth getting it assessed.

## Loading the tendon

Physiotherapists usually treat Achilles tendinopathy with a calf-strengthening programme that gets gradually harder, rather than with rest alone.

The best-known early study gave 15 recreational runners with long-standing Achilles pain 12 weeks of heavy eccentric calf training, slowly lowering the heel under load. All of them returned to running. It was a small study without a randomised comparison group<Cite id="I17" />. Since then, physiotherapists have used a range of heavy, slow calf exercises.

The <TopicLink to="strength-mobility-drills/strength-training">Strength library</TopicLink> shows the straight-knee and bent-knee calf raises. A physiotherapist can set how heavy, how many and how fast, and how to progress.

## Running while it settles

In a trial of 38 people with Achilles tendinopathy, those who kept running and jumping within a pain limit did as well as those who rested from running and jumping for 6 weeks<Cite id="I2" />. The limits were:

- Pain during and after activity no higher than **5 out of 10**.
- Pain **settled by the next morning**.
- Pain and stiffness **not rising from week to week**.

If you go over those limits, ease back. Physiotherapists often suggest leaving out hills, fast running and springy drills at first, as they ask more of the calf and tendon. See <TopicLink to="injuries-health/soreness-or-pain">soreness or pain?</TopicLink>

<Box kind="note" title="Be patient">

Tendon problems can be slow to settle. The calf-training programme in the study above ran for 12 weeks<Cite id="I17" />. Physiotherapists usually advise keeping up the calf exercises after the pain has gone, and building your running back up gradually. See <TopicLink to="injuries-health/coming-back">coming back after injury</TopicLink>.

</Box>
