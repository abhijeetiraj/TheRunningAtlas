**Follow your clinician's plan** if you've had a bone stress injury, a fracture or surgery, or were told to keep weight off the leg. Some bone stress injuries are at high-risk sites, and these need specialist care and their own timeline<Cite id="I14" />. See <TopicLink to="injuries-health/bone-stress">bone stress injuries</TopicLink>.

**Stop and get it checked** if the pain comes back in one spot, makes you limp, or is there at rest or at night. See <TopicLink to="injuries-health/when-to-get-checked">when to stop and get checked</TopicLink>.

</Box>

## While you're out

Fitness fades quickly. In highly trained athletes, less than 4 weeks of stopped or much reduced training brought a rapid fall in VO2max and blood volume<Cite id="T5" />.

Cross-training can hold on to some of it. A review found that trained endurance athletes kept their aerobic performance with deep-water running for up to 6 weeks<Cite id="T6" />. Cycling, swimming and other options are covered in <TopicLink to="training/cross-training">cross-training</TopicLink>. Pick one that doesn't hurt the injury, and agree it with your physiotherapist.

## When to start again

A physiotherapist can tell you when you're ready, and may use simple tests of strength and how well the injured leg takes load.

## Restart with run and walk

You don't have to pick up where you left off. A short run and walk session is a gentle way back in, the same way beginner plans such as Couch to 5K start, with short runs between walks<Cite id="S1" />. The <TopicLink to="start-running/first-run">first run</TopicLink> page has a run and walk planner.

- **Keep it easy**: a pace you could talk at. See <TopicLink to="start-running/talk-test">the talk test</TopicLink>.
- **Run on alternate days** at first, so there's a day between to see how it responds.
- **Choose flat, even ground**, and leave out hills, speed work and springy drills until you're back to regular easy running.

## Build up gradually

In novice runners, those whose weekly distance rose by more than 30% had more of some overuse injuries than those whose distance rose by less than 10%. That's a link, not proof of cause<Cite id="S5" />. Coming back after injury is a good time to be cautious. See <TopicLink to="training/building-up">building up gradually</TopicLink>.

Use the pain scale to guide you<Cite id="I2" />:

- Pain during and after running stays at **5 or below out of 10**.
- It has **settled by the next morning**.
- It **doesn't creep up** from week to week.

If it goes over those limits, drop back to the last level that was fine. That scale was tested for Achilles pain, so agree it with a physiotherapist. See <TopicLink to="injuries-health/soreness-or-pain">soreness or pain?</TopicLink>

<Box kind="note" title="Keep the rehab going">

The exercises that helped you recover are worth keeping up once you're running again. Fold them into your regular <TopicLink to="strength-mobility-drills/strength-training">strength training</TopicLink>.

</Box>
