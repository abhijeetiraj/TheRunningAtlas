<Box kind="care">

**Seek care now** if the pain started after a fall, a jump or a twist, or you can't put weight on the foot.

**Get it checked soon** if the heel bone itself hurts when squeezed from the sides, the pain is there at rest or at night, you have numbness, tingling or swelling, or it isn't improving after a few weeks of easing back. These can point to something other than plantar heel pain, such as a bone stress injury. See <TopicLink to="injuries-health/bone-stress">bone stress injuries</TopicLink>.

</Box>

## What it is

The plantar fascia is a thick band of tissue under the foot, running from the heel bone to the base of the toes. Plantar heel pain, often called plantar fasciitis, is pain under the heel or in the arch. It's typically worst with the first steps in the morning, or when you get up after sitting<Cite id="I10" />.

Pooling studies of runners, it affected about 5 to 10% of runners during follow-up, making it one of the most common running injuries<Cite id="I1" />.

Most cases get better, though some last a long time<Cite id="I10" />. That can be frustrating, so it helps to know it's often slow.

## What helps

In a trial of 48 people with plantar fasciitis, all of whom were given shoe inserts, one group added a high-load strength exercise and the other added daily stretching<Cite id="I11" />:

- **The exercise** was a single-leg heel raise with a rolled towel under the toes, so the toes bend up as the heel rises. It was done every other day.
- **At 3 months**, the strength group had better foot function.
- **At 1, 6 and 12 months**, there was no difference between the groups.

So in that trial, strength training brought better function sooner, but not a better result in the end. It was one small trial. The <TopicLink to="strength-mobility-drills/strength-training">Strength library</TopicLink> shows the straight-knee calf raise it's built on. A physiotherapist can show you the towel version and set the load.

## Running while it settles

You may not have to stop running completely. Ease back to a level that doesn't make the pain worse:

- **Run less**, and swap some runs for <TopicLink to="training/cross-training">cross-training</TopicLink> if running flares it.
- **Use the pain scale**: pain during and after running stays mild, has settled by the next morning, and doesn't creep up from week to week. See <TopicLink to="injuries-health/soreness-or-pain">soreness or pain?</TopicLink> That scale was tested for Achilles pain, so agree it with a physiotherapist.
- **Build back gradually** once it settles. See <TopicLink to="injuries-health/coming-back">coming back after injury</TopicLink>.
