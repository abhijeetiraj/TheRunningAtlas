<Box kind="care" title="Altitude illness: act now">

**Call emergency services**, and go down if you safely can, if someone at altitude becomes confused, loses their coordination (stumbling, can't walk straight) or is breathless even at rest. These can be signs of swelling of the brain or lungs, which are emergencies<Cite id="E38" />.

**Stop and seek medical advice** if you get a headache with nausea, tiredness or dizziness after going high. That can be acute mountain sickness<Cite id="E38" />. Don't run and don't go any higher. If it gets worse, seek care now and go down.

</Box>

## Why the air is "thinner"

The air holds about 21% oxygen at any height. What changes is the air pressure. Higher up, there's less air above you pressing down, so the pressure falls, and with it the pressure pushing oxygen from your lungs into your blood. At 2,500 m, it is about three-quarters of the sea-level value<Cite id="E35" />.

## What it does to your running

With less oxygen getting into the blood, there's less to deliver to the muscles. See <TopicLink to="the-engine/heart-lungs-oxygen">heart, lungs and oxygen</TopicLink>. In 8 endurance-trained athletes tested in a low-pressure chamber at simulated altitudes from 300 to 2,800 m, VO2max fell by about 6.3% for every 1,000 m<Cite id="E36" />.

In practice, that means:

- **Easy runs feel harder.** The same pace takes a bigger share of your fitness, so you breathe harder and your heart rate is higher.
- **Long, hard efforts are slower.** With a lower VO2max, you can't hold your sea-level pace for as long.

Slow down and run by effort, for example with the <TopicLink to="start-running/talk-test">talk test</TopicLink>, rather than trying to hold your sea-level pace.

## Altitude training

Over weeks at altitude, the body adapts, partly by making more red blood cells<Cite id="E37" />. Some athletes use this to try to race faster at sea level. The catch is that training hard at altitude is harder, because you can't run as fast.

One well-known study split 39 competitive runners into three groups for 4 weeks. Those who lived at 2,500 m but trained at 1,250 m improved their 5,000 m time by about 13 seconds. Those who lived and trained high, or lived and trained low, did not<Cite id="E37" />. This "live high, train low" idea has been studied a lot since.

## Travelling high to run

<Box kind="try" title="When you go up">

- Keep the first few days easy and short. Expect slower paces.
- Above about 3,000 m, raise the height you sleep at by no more than about 500 m a day<Cite id="E38" />.
- Rest more in the first days, and don't push through feeling unwell.
- If you have a heart or lung condition, are pregnant, or take regular medicines, talk to your doctor before you go high.

</Box>
