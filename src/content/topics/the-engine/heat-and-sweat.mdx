<Box kind="care" title="Heat illness: act now">

**Call emergency services** if a runner in the heat is confused, behaves oddly, collapses or has a seizure. That can be heat stroke, which is a medical emergency. Start cooling them straight away while you wait, ideally in cold water<Cite id="E33" />.

**Stop running and seek care now** if you have any one of these: feeling faint or dizzy, unusual weakness, a headache, nausea or vomiting<Cite id="E44" />. Get into the shade, cool down and don't carry on. If you feel worse, or don't start to feel better soon after stopping, get help.

</Box>

## How you cool down

Working muscles make a lot of heat. Your body moves it to the skin with extra blood flow, and gets rid of it mainly by sweating: as sweat evaporates, it takes heat with it.

That works well in cool, dry air. When it's hot, there's less difference between your skin and the air. When it's humid, sweat evaporates more slowly and drips off without cooling you as much. Sun, no wind and dark clothing all add to the load.

## Heat slows you down

In results from several big-city marathons over many years, finishing times slowed as the weather got warmer, and slower runners were slowed more than the fastest<Cite id="E32" />. One likely reason is that slower runners are out for longer, as the day warms up.

Your heart rate also climbs more on a hot day. On long, steady runs it drifts up while the blood pumped per beat falls, and heat and dehydration can make that drift larger<Cite id="E18" />. Running at your usual pace in the heat is really running harder. See <TopicLink to="the-engine/heart-rate-zones">heart-rate zones</TopicLink>.

## Getting used to the heat

Your body adapts to regular exercise in the heat. Much of the change comes in the first week, and most of it within about two weeks<Cite id="E31" />. You start sweating sooner and more, and your heart rate and body temperature stay lower at the same effort<Cite id="E31" />.

Exercising hard in the heat before you are used to it raises the risk of heat illness<Cite id="E44" />. So when it turns hot, or you travel somewhere hotter, keep runs shorter and easier for the first week or two and build up.

<Box kind="try" title="On hot days">

- Run early in the morning or in the evening, when it's cooler, and look for shade.
- Wear light, loose, light-coloured clothes and a hat or visor.
- Slow down. Go by effort and breathing, not pace.
- Carry water on longer runs, or plan a route past places to drink.
- Ease off or stop if you feel unwell. A run can always be cut short.
- If you take regular medicines or have a health condition, ask your doctor or pharmacist about exercising in the heat.

</Box>

## Sweat rate: learn yours

How much people sweat, and how salty their sweat is, varies a lot from person to person, so a drinking plan should be your own. Weighing yourself before and after exercise is a simple way to estimate your sweat loss<Cite id="E30" />.

<SweatRate />

A common aim is to avoid losing more than about 2% of your body weight through water loss; more dehydration than that can make heat strain worse and hurt endurance performance<Cite id="E30" />. Measure on a few runs in different weather, since your rate changes with heat, pace and how used to the heat you are.

## Don't drink too much

Drinking far more than you sweat is dangerous too. It can lower the sodium in your blood, called hyponatraemia. In 488 runners at the 2002 Boston Marathon who gave a blood sample at the finish, 13% had low blood sodium. Gaining weight during the race, a sign of drinking more than was lost, was the strongest link<Cite id="E34" />.

So drink to replace what you lose, not more. If you weigh more after a run than before, you drank too much. Severe low blood sodium can cause confusion and seizures and needs emergency treatment<Cite id="E45" />: call emergency services. See <TopicLink to="fuel-recovery/fluids-sodium">fluids and sodium</TopicLink> and <TopicLink to="injuries-health/red-flags">red flags</TopicLink>.
