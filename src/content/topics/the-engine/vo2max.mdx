## A ceiling on oxygen

As you run faster, you use more oxygen. But not forever: at some point, working harder no longer raises the oxygen you use. That ceiling is your maximal oxygen uptake, or VO2max. The idea goes back to A. V. Hill and colleagues in the 1920s<Cite id="E6" />.

VO2max is given in millilitres of oxygen per minute, or per kilogram of body mass per minute (mL/kg/min) so that people of different sizes can be compared. You can see how it builds up in the <TopicLink to="the-engine/heart-lungs-oxygen">oxygen journey</TopicLink> lab: at "All out", oxygen use reaches its top.

## What sets it

Oxygen has to be breathed in, carried by the blood, pumped by the heart and used by the muscles. In trained people running, the main limit is usually delivery: how much oxygen the heart and blood can get to the muscles<Cite id="E4" />. That's why VO2max rises when training makes the heart pump more blood with each beat<Cite id="E5" />.

## How it's measured

The accurate way is a lab test: you run on a treadmill that gets faster or steeper every minute or two, wearing a mask that measures the air you breathe, until you can't go on.

Many sports watches show an estimated VO2max from your heart rate and pace. It's an estimate, not a measurement, and it depends on the watch knowing your heart rate well. It's most useful for watching your own trend over months, rather than as an exact number. See <TopicLink to="the-engine/heart-rate-zones">heart-rate zones</TopicLink> for how accurate wrist heart rate is.

## Training raises it, by different amounts

Pooling 28 studies of healthy adults aged 18 to 45, both high-intensity interval training and continuous endurance training raised VO2max a lot compared with no training. Intervals gave a possibly small extra gain<Cite id="E9" />.

How much any one person gains varies widely. In a study of about 480 sedentary adults from 99 families who all did the same 20-week programme, VO2max rose by about 400 mL a minute on average. But some people gained a lot and others very little, and gains ran in families: up to 47% of the variation was estimated to be inherited<Cite id="E7" />.

So if a friend's fitness jumps faster than yours on the same plan, that's normal. Everyone who trains still gets other benefits, and VO2max is not the only thing that improves.

## Age

VO2max falls as we get older, and the fall speeds up. In 810 healthy adults followed for about 8 years, it fell by about 3 to 6% per decade in their 20s and 30s, and by more than 20% per decade from their 70s. More active people were fitter at every age<Cite id="E8" />.

## Not the whole story

VO2max is the size of the engine. Two other things decide how fast you can race:

- **How much of it you can hold** for the length of the race. That depends on your <TopicLink to="the-engine/lactate-thresholds">thresholds</TopicLink>.
- **Running economy**: how much oxygen each kilometre costs you. Among runners with similar VO2max, economy explained much of who ran faster<Cite id="H14" />. See <TopicLink to="how-running-works/running-economy">running economy</TopicLink>.
