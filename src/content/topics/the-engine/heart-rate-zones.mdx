## Why heart rate tracks effort

The harder you run, the more oxygen your muscles need, and the faster your heart beats to deliver it. That makes heart rate a useful window on effort. See <TopicLink to="the-engine/heart-lungs-oxygen">heart, lungs and oxygen</TopicLink>.

Most zone systems start from your maximal heart rate, and that's where the trouble begins.

## Your maximum: formulas are rough

The best-known formula is "220 minus age". It didn't come from a study designed to produce it, and it was never an accurate guide for individuals<Cite id="E14" />.

A large analysis of 351 studies, checked in a lab study of 514 people, gave a better formula: **208 − 0.7 × age**. "220 minus age" underestimated maximal heart rate in older adults. Even so, the new formula had a standard error of about 10 beats a minute<Cite id="E15" />. So for any one person, the formula can easily be 10 or more beats out.

If you have seen your heart rate near its top in a hard race or a very hard effort, that real number beats any formula.

## Heart rate reserve

Your heart rate reserve is the gap between your maximal and your resting heart rate. A training intensity can be set as resting heart rate plus a share of that gap<Cite id="E16" />. This uses two of your own numbers instead of one, so it fits people with low or high resting heart rates better than a plain share of the maximum.

The American College of Sports Medicine uses these ranges of heart rate reserve: 30 to 39% light, 40 to 59% moderate, 60 to 89% vigorous and 90% or more near maximal<Cite id="E17" />.

## Five-zone systems

Watches and apps often split effort into five zones. They don't all draw the lines in the same place, so "zone 2" on one watch may not match "zone 2" on another, or in a training plan. Check what your zones are based on before following a plan that uses them.

## Heart rate drifts

On a long, steady run, your heart rate slowly rises even at the same pace, while the blood pumped per beat falls. This is called cardiovascular drift. Heat and dehydration can make it larger<Cite id="E18" />. So holding a fixed heart rate on a long or hot run means slowing down as you go, which is often the right thing to do. See <TopicLink to="the-engine/heat-and-sweat">heat and sweat</TopicLink>.

## How accurate is your sensor?

In 50 adults tested on a treadmill, a bike and an elliptical, a chest-strap monitor agreed most closely with an ECG. Wrist-worn optical monitors were less accurate, most of all on the elliptical<Cite id="E19" />. A wrist reading that suddenly jumps, or sits at the same number as your steps per minute, can be a sign of a bad reading.

## Use it with feel

Heart rate is a good guide, not a rule. On days when you are tired, hot, stressed or unwell, the same pace can bring a higher heart rate. Check zones against how you feel, for example with the <TopicLink to="start-running/talk-test">talk test</TopicLink>, and against your <TopicLink to="the-engine/lactate-thresholds">thresholds</TopicLink>.

<Box kind="care" title="Stop and seek care now">

Stop running and seek care straight away if your heart races or beats irregularly and doesn't settle when you stop, or comes with chest pain, breathlessness, dizziness or faintness. For chest pain, collapse or confusion, call emergency services. A watch reading can't tell you that you are fine: go by your symptoms. If you have a heart condition or symptoms, check with a doctor before doing an all-out effort to find your maximum. See <TopicLink to="start-running/see-a-doctor-first">when to see a doctor first</TopicLink>.
