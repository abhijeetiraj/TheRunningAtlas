## Lactate is a fuel

For a long time lactate (often called "lactic acid") was blamed for tired, burning legs and thought of as a waste product. That picture is out of date. Muscles, the heart and the brain take up lactate and use it as fuel, and the liver can turn it back into glucose<Cite id="E10" />.

Your muscles make lactate all the time when they break down glucose, even at rest. What matters is the balance between how fast it is made and how fast it is used. See <TopicLink to="the-engine/energy-systems">energy systems</TopicLink>.

## What happens as you speed up

If you ran a series of stages on a treadmill, each a little faster, and had a drop of blood tested after each one, you'd usually see something like this:

1. **Easy running.** Lactate stays close to resting levels. It is made and used at about the same rate.
2. **Steady to moderately hard.** Lactate starts to rise above rest, but can level off if you hold the pace.
3. **Hard.** Past a certain point, lactate keeps climbing the longer you hold the pace, and you have to slow down or stop.

The efforts where these changes happen are called thresholds. There are many different definitions and tests, and they don't all measure the same thing. Even so, a review found them useful for setting training and predicting endurance performance<Cite id="E11" />.

## Critical speed

Critical speed is a threshold you can estimate from race results alone. The idea comes from a simple model: in an all-out effort, the distance you cover rises in a straight line with how long you run. Distance equals critical speed times time, plus a fixed reserve of distance, called D′ ("D prime"), that you can run above critical speed<Cite id="E12" />.

Critical speed marks a real boundary. Below it, oxygen use and blood lactate settle to a steady level. Above it, they keep rising until you have to stop<Cite id="E13" />.

## Feeling your thresholds

You don't need a lab. Your breathing changes as effort rises, and gives a rough guide:

- **Easy:** you can talk in full sentences.
- **Around the first threshold:** talking in full sentences stops being comfortable. In lab tests, this point sat close to the ventilatory threshold, a marker between moderate and hard exercise<Cite id="S7" />. See <TopicLink to="start-running/talk-test">the talk test</TopicLink>.
- **Near critical speed:** only a few words at a time.

Heart rate is another guide, though it drifts up on long runs and in the heat. See <TopicLink to="the-engine/heart-rate-zones">heart-rate zones</TopicLink>.

## Using thresholds in training

Many training plans are built around these boundaries: much of the running at an easy, conversational effort below the first one, and harder sessions around or above critical speed, for limited time.
