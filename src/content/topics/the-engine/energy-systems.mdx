## ATP: the energy your muscles spend

Every muscle contraction is powered by a molecule called ATP. When a muscle uses it, ATP breaks apart and releases energy. Muscles hold only a small amount, so they have to keep remaking it as fast as they use it. How they do that is what people mean by "energy systems".

## Three ways to remake ATP

Muscles remake ATP in three ways<Cite id="E1" />:

<div class="table-scroll">

| Way | What it uses | How fast | How long it lasts |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Phosphocreatine | A small store in the muscle | Fastest | Seconds |
| Glycolysis, without oxygen | Glucose and glycogen | Fast | Short, hard efforts |
| Aerobic, with oxygen | Carbohydrate and fat, in the mitochondria | Slower to rise | Hours |

</div>

- **Phosphocreatine** is a ready-made reserve. It gives a burst of energy almost instantly, for a jump, a sprint start or the first seconds of a hard effort, but it runs low quickly.
- **Glycolysis** breaks down glucose without needing oxygen. It is fast and makes lactate along the way. Lactate is not waste; your body uses it as fuel. See <TopicLink to="the-engine/lactate-thresholds">lactate and thresholds</TopicLink>.
- **Aerobic energy** happens inside the mitochondria, using oxygen brought by your blood. It is slower to get going but can burn carbohydrate and fat for hours. See <TopicLink to="the-engine/heart-lungs-oxygen">heart, lungs and oxygen</TopicLink>.

## They work together, not in turns

A common picture shows the three systems taking turns: phosphocreatine for the first seconds, then glycolysis, then aerobic. That's not quite right. All three work at once in almost every effort; what changes is how much each one gives<Cite id="E1" />.

In an all-out effort, the aerobic and non-aerobic systems supply equal shares of the energy at somewhere between one and two minutes, most probably at about 75 seconds<Cite id="E2" />. So even a flat-out effort of a minute or so gets a large share of its energy from oxygen, and anything longer than a couple of minutes, from a mile to a marathon, gets most of it that way.

<Box kind="note" title="What this means for runners">

For almost any race distance most runners do, the aerobic system is the main engine. That's why so much of endurance training is about building it: a heart that pumps more with each beat<Cite id="E5" />, more capillaries, and muscles with more mitochondria<Cite id="E23" />. See <TopicLink to="the-engine/vo2max">VO2max</TopicLink> and <TopicLink to="the-engine/muscle-fibres">muscle fibres</TopicLink>.

</Box>

## Where the fuel comes from

The aerobic system can burn both fat and carbohydrate. Fat stores are huge; carbohydrate stores are small. The harder you run, the more you lean on carbohydrate<Cite id="E25" />. See <TopicLink to="the-engine/carbs-and-fat">carbs and fat as fuel</TopicLink>.
