## The journey, step by step

Use the step buttons in the lab above to follow a red blood cell around the loop.

1. **Lungs.** You breathe air into millions of tiny air sacs. Oxygen crosses a very thin wall into the blood, where red blood cells carry it on a protein called haemoglobin.
2. **Heart.** The left side of the heart pumps the oxygen-rich blood out to the body. The right side takes blood coming back and sends it to the lungs.
3. **Arteries.** The blood flows out to the working muscles. When you run, more of it goes to the legs.
4. **Muscle.** In tiny vessels called capillaries, oxygen leaves the blood and moves into the muscle fibres. There, the mitochondria use it to make energy. See <TopicLink to="the-engine/energy-systems">energy systems</TopicLink>.
5. **Back to the heart.** The blood, now carrying less oxygen and more carbon dioxide, returns to the heart and goes on to the lungs, where you breathe the carbon dioxide out.

## The Fick principle

There is a neat way to sum up the whole loop. The oxygen your body uses each minute equals:

**the blood your heart pumps each minute** × **the oxygen each litre of blood gives up on its way round**<Cite id="E3" />

The first part, called cardiac output, is your heart rate times the blood pumped with each beat. The second is the difference between the oxygen in the blood leaving the lungs and in the blood coming back.

Move the effort slider in the lab and watch both parts rise. In the lab's model of a fit 70 kg adult, going from rest to all out:

- the heart's output rises from about 5 to about 21 litres a minute, as heart rate and the blood per beat both go up;
- each litre of blood gives up about three times as much oxygen;
- so the oxygen used rises more than tenfold.

These are typical values chosen for the model, not measurements of you<Cite id="E3" />.

## What sets the limit?

As you run harder, your oxygen use eventually stops rising. That ceiling is your <TopicLink to="the-engine/vo2max">VO2max</TopicLink>. What sets it?

Oxygen has to pass through every step of the journey, so any of them could, in principle, be the bottleneck. For trained people exercising with large muscle groups, as in running, the main limit is usually how much oxygen the heart and blood can deliver, rather than how much the muscles can use<Cite id="E4" />.

## How training changes the heart

Endurance athletes can pump far more blood per minute at their maximum than untrained people. That's mainly because each beat pumps more blood. Maximal heart rate hardly changes with training<Cite id="E5" />.

So a trained heart reaches the same output with fewer beats. It's one reason a fit runner's heart rate is lower at the same easy pace, and why heart rate is a useful, if rough, guide to effort. See <TopicLink to="the-engine/heart-rate-zones">heart-rate zones</TopicLink>.

<Box kind="care" title="Stop and seek care now">

Stop running and seek care straight away if you have any one of these: pain, pressure or tightness in your chest, or pain spreading to your arm, neck or jaw; breathlessness that doesn't settle within a few minutes of stopping; feeling faint or dizzy; or a racing or irregular heartbeat that doesn't settle. For chest pain, collapse or confusion, call emergency services. See <TopicLink to="start-running/see-a-doctor-first">when to see a doctor first</TopicLink>.
