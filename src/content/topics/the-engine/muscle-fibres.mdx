## What a muscle is made of

Cut across a calf muscle, as the lab above does, and you see thousands of long, thin cells called muscle fibres, packed side by side. Each fibre runs along the muscle and shortens to pull on the tendon.

Not all fibres are the same. Roughly, there are three kinds:

- **Type I, slow.** They contract slowly and tire very slowly. They are packed with mitochondria and surrounded by many capillaries, so they are good at using oxygen.
- **Type IIa, fast.** Faster and more powerful, and still fairly resistant to fatigue.
- **Type IIx, fastest.** The fastest and most powerful, but they tire quickly. They rely more on energy made without oxygen.

The table next to the lab sums this up. Every muscle has a mix of all three, scattered through it.

## Motor units and the size principle

Fibres don't switch on one at a time. Each nerve cell that drives a muscle, a motor neuron, connects to a group of fibres of one type. A nerve cell and its fibres together are called a motor unit. When the nerve fires, all its fibres contract.

When you need more force, your nervous system switches on more motor units, in order of size: smallest first. Small motor units have slow fibres; bigger ones have fast fibres. So slow, fatigue-resistant units switch on first, and bigger, faster ones join as the force needed rises. This is called the size principle<Cite id="E20" />.

Move the effort slider in the lab to see it. On an easy run, mostly slow fibres are working. On a hard run, fast IIa fibres join them. Only near a sprint are the fastest fibres switched on.

## Distance runners and sprinters

People differ in their fibre mix. In one 1970s study, calf muscle samples from 14 elite distance runners averaged 79% slow-twitch fibres<Cite id="E21" />. The lab's "Mostly slow" mix is set close to that. Among college track athletes, sprinters had a larger share of fast-twitch fibres in the calf than distance runners<Cite id="E22" />.

Those are small groups of top athletes. Most people have a mix somewhere in between, and your fibre type is only one of many things that shape how you run. You don't need a muscle sample to choose your event.

## Training changes muscle

Training doesn't simply turn fast fibres into slow ones, but it does change what fibres can do. In a classic study, rats trained to run had muscles whose capacity to use oxygen roughly doubled<Cite id="E23" />. Endurance training builds more mitochondria and capillaries, so fibres can keep working longer on oxygen.

This is part of why easy running matters: it trains the slow fibres you use most, and the fast IIa fibres you recruit as runs get harder or longer and fibres tire.
