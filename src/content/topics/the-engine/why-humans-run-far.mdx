## Slow sprinters, good endurance runners

Over a short dash, humans are slow next to many four-legged animals. But over long distances, and especially in the heat, humans do surprisingly well. That raises a question: is our body built for it?

## The endurance running idea

In 2004, two researchers listed many features of the human body that help endurance running but matter little for walking. They argued that endurance running helped shape human evolution<Cite id="E39" />. Some of those features:

- **Springy tendons in the legs and feet.** The Achilles tendon and the arch of the foot stretch and recoil with each step, and tendons return most of the energy used to stretch them<Cite id="H3" />. See <TopicLink to="how-running-works/springs-and-tendons">springs and tendons</TopicLink>.
- **A ligament that steadies the head.** It helps keep the head from bobbing as you run.
- **A large gluteus maximus.** Our biggest muscle works far harder in running than in walking. In running it helps keep the trunk from pitching forward with each step<Cite id="E40" />.
- **Arms and shoulders that swing freely** to balance the twist of the legs. See <TopicLink to="how-running-works/arms-posture">arms, posture and trunk</TopicLink>.

## Sweating: the key to running in the heat

Many four-legged mammals cool down mainly by panting, which is hard to combine with galloping. Humans lose heat by sweating over almost the whole body, so we can keep running in the heat<Cite id="E42" />. Having little body hair may help the sweat evaporate.

That's also why heat is such a big deal for runners today: sweating is our main way of keeping cool, and it has limits. See <TopicLink to="the-engine/heat-and-sweat">heat and sweat</TopicLink>.

## Persistence hunting

How might endurance have helped our ancestors? One idea is persistence hunting: chasing an animal through the heat, tracking it whenever it runs off, until it is too hot to go on. It has been recorded among hunter-gatherers in the Kalahari<Cite id="E41" />.

## Still debated

The endurance running idea is well argued, but it's not settled. Scientists still debate how important running was compared with walking, scavenging and other ways of finding food, and how often persistence hunting was really used. Some features on the list may have evolved for other reasons and only later helped running.

Whatever the full story, your body has the equipment for running long distances. Like any skill it has to be trained, and built up gradually. See <TopicLink to="start-running/how-often-how-far">how often and how far</TopicLink>.
