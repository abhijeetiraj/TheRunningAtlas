## "Stretch before you run, or you'll get injured"

In more than 1,500 army recruits, adding short static stretches to the warm-up made no meaningful difference to injury risk<Cite id="W1" />. In more than 2,700 runners, in a study reported at a conference, a 3 to 5 minute stretch before running neither prevented nor caused injury<Cite id="W2" />. Pooling 25 trials, stretching programmes showed no clear protective effect, while strength training programmes were linked with the biggest drop in injuries<Cite id="W5" />.

Long static holds just before exercise can even cut performance a little, while dynamic stretching gave a small boost<Cite id="W3" />. So warm up by moving, not by holding stretches. See the <TopicLink to="strength-mobility-drills/warm-up">warm-up studio</TopicLink>.

## "Stretching after a run stops you getting sore"

A Cochrane review of randomised trials found that stretching before, after, or before and after exercise did not produce clinically important reductions in delayed muscle soreness. Stretching after exercise lowered soreness a day later by about 1 point on a 100-point scale<Cite id="M6" />. Stretch because you like it or to keep your range of movement, not to stop soreness. See <TopicLink to="strength-mobility-drills/stretching">stretching after runs</TopicLink>.

## "Running wears out your knees"

Pooling 17 studies of 114,829 people, hip and knee osteoarthritis was found in about 3.5% of recreational runners, against 10.2% of people who didn't run and 13.3% of competitive (elite or international) runners<Cite id="L1" />. These are links, not proof: people who keep running may differ in other ways from those who don't. But they don't fit the idea that recreational running wears out your joints.

## "You should land on your forefoot"

Most runners land heel first. Of 283 runners filmed at the 15 km point of an elite-level half marathon, about 75% did<Cite id="H5" />, and so did about 89% of 936 runners filmed in another road race<Cite id="R9" />. A review of 53 studies couldn't tell whether foot strike changes injury risk, and switching to a forefoot strike lowered running economy in the short term<Cite id="R7" />.

Changing how you land moves the load rather than removing it: in one study of 38 women, forefoot strikers had lower knee loading but higher Achilles tendon force than heel strikers<Cite id="R6" />. See <TopicLink to="how-running-works/foot-strike">foot strike</TopicLink>.

## "Everyone should run at 180 steps a minute"

The number comes from coach Jack Daniels' count of elite runners' cadence at the 1984 Olympics, not from a study of what suits each runner<Cite id="P1" />. In 10 recreational runners, the stride length each chose freely was close to the one that used the least oxygen<Cite id="H7" />.

A small change can still help some runners: raising step rate by 5 to 10% at the same speed reduced loading at the knee and hip<Cite id="R8" />. But there's no number everyone should hit. See <TopicLink to="how-running-works/cadence-step-length">cadence and step length</TopicLink> and the <TopicLink to="tools/cadence-counter">cadence counter</TopicLink>.

## "Lactate is a waste product"

Lactate is a fuel. Muscles, the heart and the brain take it up and use it, and the liver can turn it back into glucose<Cite id="E10" />. Its rise in the blood as you run harder marks useful training thresholds. See <TopicLink to="the-engine/lactate-thresholds">lactate and thresholds</TopicLink>.

## "You need shoes for your foot type"

In 927 novice runners given the same neutral shoe and followed for a year, runners whose feet rolled inwards did not have more injuries than runners with neutral feet<Cite id="S11" />. Across trials in military recruits, choosing running shoes by the height of the foot arch did not reduce injuries<Cite id="S12" />. See <TopicLink to="start-running/shoes-and-kit">shoes and kit that matter</TopicLink>.

## "Runners don't need strength training"

A review of 24 studies in middle- and long-distance runners found that adding strength training generally improved running economy by about 2 to 8%, and tended to improve time-trial performance over 1.5 to 10 km<Cite id="M1" />. And in the pooled trials above, strength training programmes were linked with the biggest drop in injuries<Cite id="W5" />. See <TopicLink to="strength-mobility-drills/strength-training">strength training for runners</TopicLink>.

## "Drink as much as you can in a race"

The consensus statement advises using thirst to guide how much to drink. Drinking more than you lose, over hours, is the main cause of low blood sodium (exercise-associated hyponatraemia) in races<Cite id="C4" />. Drink to thirst. See <TopicLink to="racing/race-day-fuel">race-day fuel</TopicLink>.

## "The 10% rule keeps you injury-free"

In a trial with 532 novice runners, a 13-week programme built on the 10% rule had about as many injuries as a standard 8-week programme<Cite id="S4" />. Big jumps are a different matter: in 873 novice runners, weekly increases of more than 30% went with more of some overuse injuries, a link, not proof<Cite id="S5" />. So build gradually, but 10% isn't magic. See <TopicLink to="training/building-up">building up gradually</TopicLink>.

## "Hard landings cause injuries"

It's not that simple. A review of 18 studies found no difference in peak vertical ground forces between injured runners and others. How quickly the force rose was higher in runners with a history of stress fractures, mostly in studies looking back<Cite id="H11" />. See <TopicLink to="how-running-works/forces-impact">forces and impact</TopicLink>.

## "Super shoes make you 4% faster"

In 18 high-calibre runners in a lab, a prototype racing shoe with a curved carbon plate and a new foam lowered the energy cost of running by about 4% on average, against two established racing shoes<Cite id="H18" />. That's a saving in energy at a set speed for fast runners, not a promise of a 4% faster race for everyone. See <TopicLink to="how-running-works/shoe-science">shoe science</TopicLink>.

## "Your 10K time tells you your marathon time"

In a survey of 2,303 recreational runners, the Riegel formula predicted times well up to the half marathon, but its marathon predictions were too fast: by at least 10 minutes for half of the runners<Cite id="P4" />. A marathon depends on endurance and fuel as well as speed. See the <TopicLink to="tools/race-time-estimator">race-time estimator</TopicLink>.
