## What the arms are doing

Each time a leg swings forward, it twists the body around its long axis. Left alone, your shoulders and pelvis would rock from side to side with every step. The arms swing the other way, left arm with right leg, and cancel much of that twist.

You can feel this. Try jogging a few steps with your hands clasped behind your back, and your shoulders start to rotate to make up for it.

In one lab study, runners ran with their arms held behind the back, across the chest or on top of the head. Compared with running normally, those took about 3%, 9% and 13% more energy. Without arm swing, the runners twisted their shoulders and pelvis more<Cite id="H12" />. So a normal arm swing saves energy rather than wasting it.

## How to hold your arms

Most runners already swing their arms in a way that works for them. Common coaching cues, which are sensible but not well tested, are:

- **Elbows bent**, roughly at a right angle, though good runners vary.
- **Swing from the shoulder**, mostly forward and back. A little crossing in front of the body is normal; it is part of balancing the legs.
- **Loose hands**, as if holding something light. Clenched fists tend to tense the arms and shoulders.
- **Shoulders down**, away from the ears. Some runners hunch as they tire.

You can watch the arms counter the legs in the <TopicLink to="how-running-works/stride">stride lab</TopicLink>: slow the runner right down and turn the camera to look from above.

## Posture and trunk lean

A tall posture, with your head over your shoulders and your hips under you, is a good default. Most runners lean the trunk slightly forward, and lean more when they speed up or run uphill.

How much you lean shifts where the work happens. In one study, running with the trunk leaning further forward lowered the stress at the joint behind the kneecap and shifted work towards the hip<Cite id="H13" />. That may help some runners with pain at the front of the knee, but it asks more of the hip and trunk muscles. It's a trade, not a free gain.

<Box kind="try" title="Try this">

On an easy run, check in every few minutes from top to bottom: jaw loose, shoulders down, hands soft, tall through the hips. Don't force a new shape. Small reminders tend to stick better than a big change all at once.

</Box>

## The trunk's job

The trunk links the legs to the arms and keeps the pelvis steady when you are on one leg. It works hardest when you are tired, on uneven ground or on hills. Many runners add <TopicLink to="strength-mobility-drills/strength-training">strength training</TopicLink> for the hips and trunk; strength work in general has been shown to help <TopicLink to="how-running-works/running-economy">running economy</TopicLink><Cite id="H15" />.

## Should you change your form?

Your body tends to settle on a style that suits its own build. For stride length, runners left to choose freely land close to their most economical choice<Cite id="H7" />. Big, sudden changes to how you run also change where the load goes, which can bring new aches. If you want to change something, change one thing, a little, and give it weeks.

<Box kind="care">

If one arm or shoulder doesn't swing, or your posture has changed and you don't know why, have it checked. Pain that changes how you run is also worth getting checked before you try to fix your form yourself.

</Box>
