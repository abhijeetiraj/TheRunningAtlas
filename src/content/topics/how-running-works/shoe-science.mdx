## What a shoe is made of

Cut a running shoe in half from heel to toe, as the lab above does, and you find a few layers:

- **The upper**, the fabric that holds your foot.
- **The insole**, a thin removable layer under your foot.
- **The midsole**, the thick foam that does most of the work.
- **A plate**, in some shoes: a thin, stiff sheet inside the foam, often carbon fibre.
- **The outsole**, the rubber that touches the ground.

Two numbers describe the sole. **Stack** is how thick it is under the heel or under the forefoot. **Drop** is the difference: heel stack minus forefoot stack. A shoe with 34 mm at the heel and 24 mm at the forefoot has a 10 mm drop. Try the presets in the lab to see how these change the shape.

## Foam

The midsole foam squashes as you land and springs back as you push off. Foams differ in how soft they are, how much of the energy they give back, and how heavy they are. A light foam lets a shoe have a thick sole without adding much weight.

Press the shoe in the lab to see the foam squash under the heel and the forefoot. Switch off "Soft foam" to see a firmer one. The squash is an illustration, not a measurement<Cite id="H23" />.

## Plates and the "4%" shoe

In 2018, a lab study tested 18 high-calibre runners in a prototype racing shoe with a curved carbon plate and a new foam. Against two established racing shoes, it lowered the energy cost of running by about 4% on average, and it lowered it in every one of the 18<Cite id="H18" />.

Researchers are still working out how much of that saving comes from the foam, the plate and the shoe's shape. The study tested fast runners in a lab, so how much it helps slower runners is less clear. Since then, many racing shoes have used a similar mix of thick foam and a stiff plate. For what a few per cent of energy means for speed, see <TopicLink to="how-running-works/running-economy">running economy</TopicLink>.

## Racing rules

To stop shoes growing ever thicker, World Athletics rules limit the sole of a road racing shoe to 40 mm and allow no more than one rigid plate inside it<Cite id="H22" />. The lab tells you when your shoe goes over that.

## Drop and injury

Many runners wonder whether a lower drop is better. In a trial of 553 leisure-time runners given shoes with a 10, 6 or 0 mm drop and followed for 6 months, overall injury risk didn't differ by drop. Split by how much they ran, low-drop shoes went with fewer injuries in occasional runners and more in regular runners, but this was an exploratory look and needs confirming<Cite id="H19" />.

## Cushioning and injury

In a trial of 848 recreational runners given a softer or a harder version of the same shoe, the harder shoe went with a higher injury risk. Split by body mass, the difference showed only in the lighter runners<Cite id="H20" />. That's one trial, and it compared two versions of one shoe, so it doesn't tell you which soft shoe to buy.

## Using more than one pair

In 264 recreational runners followed for 22 weeks, those who used more than one pair of running shoes in turn had a lower injury risk<Cite id="H21" />. It's a link, not proof: runners who rotate shoes may differ in other ways.

## Choosing a shoe

The evidence for matching shoes to your feet hasn't held up:

- Runners whose feet roll inwards (pronate) didn't have more injuries in a neutral shoe<Cite id="S11" />.
- Assigning shoes by arch height didn't reduce injuries<Cite id="S12" />.
- A review proposed a simple "comfort filter": try several shoes and pick the one that feels most comfortable<Cite id="S10" />.

For fitting tips and when to replace shoes, see <TopicLink to="start-running/shoes-and-kit">shoes and kit</TopicLink>.

A much lower drop, a minimal shoe or a tall, soft racer changes how you land and where the load goes. Change gradually: start with short, easy runs in the new shoes and build up over a few weeks.
