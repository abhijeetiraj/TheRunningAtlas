## What it is

Put two runners on a treadmill at the same speed and measure the oxygen they breathe in. Often one needs noticeably less than the other. That runner is more economical: the same speed costs them less energy.

Economy is usually given as oxygen per kilogram of body mass, either per minute at a set speed or per kilometre. It can only be measured properly in a lab, with a mask that collects your breath.

## Why it matters

Your <TopicLink to="the-engine/vo2max">VO2max</TopicLink> is the size of your engine. Economy is how far each unit of fuel takes you. Two runners with the same engine can race very differently.

In a classic study of 12 well-trained men with similar VO2max, all among the first 19 finishers of a 10 km race, running economy explained about 65% of the differences in their race times<Cite id="H14" />. It was a small group of similar runners, so the exact number won't hold everywhere, but it shows how much economy can matter once fitness is alike.

<Box kind="model" title="A rough rule">

On the flat, the energy you spend per kilometre changes little with speed<Cite id="E28" />. So if you can keep using oxygen at the same rate, being a few per cent more economical lets you run roughly that many per cent faster. This is a simplification: air resistance, fatigue and fuel all get in the way.

</Box>

## What it depends on

Economy has no single cause. A review of trained runners listed many things that play a part at once: the body's physiology, the way it moves, training and the conditions on the day<Cite id="H16" />. Some of them:

- **Inside the muscles.** How well they turn oxygen into work, and the mix of <TopicLink to="the-engine/muscle-fibres">fibre types</TopicLink>.
- **Springs.** Tendons that store and return energy save the muscles work. See <TopicLink to="how-running-works/springs-and-tendons">springs and tendons</TopicLink>.
- **How you move.** Step length and cadence, bounce, and arm swing. Running with the arms held still cost more energy in one study<Cite id="H12" />.
- **What you carry and wear.** Body mass, the weight of your shoes, and the shoe itself.
- **Conditions.** Wind, heat, hills and the surface.

## What improves it

**Running, over months and years.** Training is one of the things that shapes economy<Cite id="H16" />, and it's slow work: muscles, tendons and movement all adapt over many months.

**Strength training.** Pooling studies of middle- and long-distance runners, strength programmes improved running economy<Cite id="H15" />. See <TopicLink to="strength-mobility-drills/strength-training">strength training</TopicLink>.

**Shoes, on race day.** In 18 high-calibre runners, a prototype shoe with a curved carbon plate and a new foam lowered the energy cost of running by about 4% on average, against two established racing shoes<Cite id="H18" />. See <TopicLink to="how-running-works/shoe-science">shoe science</TopicLink>.

**Leave your stride mostly alone.** Runners who chose their own stride length landed close to the one that used the least oxygen; much longer or shorter strides cost more<Cite id="H7" />. Forcing a new style tends to cost energy at first, even when it may help in other ways. See <TopicLink to="how-running-works/cadence-step-length">cadence and step length</TopicLink>.

<Box kind="note" title="Economy is not everything">

A very economical runner with a small engine still won't win. Race performance comes from VO2max, how much of it you can hold for the race (your <TopicLink to="the-engine/lactate-thresholds">thresholds</TopicLink>) and economy, all together.

</Box>
