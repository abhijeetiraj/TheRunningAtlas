## Three ways to land

- **Heel first (rearfoot):** the heel touches down, then the foot rolls flat.
- **Midfoot:** heel and ball of the foot land at about the same time.
- **Forefoot:** the ball of the foot lands first and the heel drops after it.

You can switch between them in the <TopicLink to="how-running-works/stride">stride lab</TopicLink> and watch the force curve change.

## How common is each?

Heel striking is the norm. In one road race, about 89% of 936 runners filmed at the 10 km mark landed heel first<Cite id="R9" />. Among 283 runners filmed 15 km into an elite-level half marathon, about 75% landed heel first, 24% on the midfoot and 1% on the forefoot, with midfoot landings more common among the faster runners<Cite id="H5" />.

So if you land on your heel, you are in good company, including many very good runners.

## What changes with each

The biggest visible difference is the start of the force curve. Heel landings usually show a sharp early spike, the impact peak; forefoot landings usually don't<Cite id="R3" />.

But load doesn't vanish, it moves. When 19 female forefoot strikers were compared with 19 matched heel strikers at the same speed, the forefoot group had lower loading at the knee but higher force in the Achilles tendon<Cite id="R6" />. Landing on the forefoot asks more of the calf and Achilles; landing on the heel asks more of the knee.

## And injuries?

This is where the evidence runs out. A review of 53 studies could not tell whether strike pattern changes injury risk, and found that switching to a forefoot strike lowered running economy in the short term<Cite id="R7" />.

One small study often quoted on the other side looked back at 52 runners from one college cross-country team: those who mostly landed heel first had about twice the rate of repetitive stress injuries<Cite id="H6" />. It's a link from a small group, looking back, not proof that changing strike prevents injury.

<Box kind="try" title="If you want to change">

There's no need to change a strike pattern that works for you. If you want to try (for example, a physiotherapist has suggested it for a knee problem):

- Change a little at a time, for a few minutes within a run, and build over weeks.
- Expect your calves and Achilles to feel it, because they take on more work.
- A slightly higher cadence often brings the foot down closer under the body without thinking about the foot at all. See <TopicLink to="how-running-works/cadence-step-length">cadence and step length</TopicLink>.

</Box>

<Box kind="care">

If changing how you run brings pain in your calf, Achilles, shin or foot that is getting worse, or that makes you limp, stop the change and get it checked.

</Box>
