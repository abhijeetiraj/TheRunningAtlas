## Why uphill costs so much

On the flat, most of your energy goes into the bounce and the push of each step. Uphill, you also have to lift your whole body against gravity, every step. That adds up fast.

Researchers have measured the energy cost of running on treadmill slopes from very steep downhill to very steep uphill. Their results fit a smooth curve, which the calculator below uses<Cite id="H17" />. On it, a 10% slope costs roughly two-thirds more energy per kilometre than the flat.

## Why gentle downhills are cheapest

Going down, gravity does some of the work for you, so the cost falls. But it doesn't keep falling. On steeper slopes you have to brake with every step to stop yourself speeding up, and braking takes energy too. On the curve, the cheapest slopes are downhills of roughly 10 to 20%; steeper than that and the cost climbs again<Cite id="H17" />.

## Run by effort, not by pace

Holding your flat pace up a hill means working much harder, and you pay for it later. The calculator shows a pace that costs about the same energy per minute as your flat pace: slower going up, faster coming down.

In practice, it's simpler to go by feel. Keep your breathing about the same as on the flat. The <TopicLink to="start-running/talk-test">talk test</TopicLink> works on hills too. Heart rate lags behind a change in effort, so it's a slow guide on short hills.

<Box kind="note" title="Treadmills">

A treadmill has no wind and the belt moves under you. For trained runners at moderate to fast speeds, a 1% incline matched the energy cost of running outdoors best<Cite id="S13" />.

## Why downhills make you sore

Going down, your thigh muscles work while they lengthen, to control your knee as it bends on landing. That kind of work, called eccentric, causes the most delayed soreness, the stiff legs you feel a day or two later<Cite id="H24" />. A hilly race or a long downhill can leave you sorer than a harder run on the flat.

The good news is that muscles adapt. After one unaccustomed bout, repeating it causes less soreness<Cite id="H25" />, so adding a little downhill running, and building it up, prepares your legs for a hilly race. See <TopicLink to="start-running/soreness">soreness</TopicLink>.

## Simple hill technique

These are common coaching cues rather than tested rules. Try them and keep what feels good.

**Going up**

- Take shorter, quicker steps, like changing to a lower gear.
- Lean into the hill a little from your ankles, not by bending at the waist.
- Drive your arms a little more. They help set the rhythm.
- On very steep climbs, walking briskly is a sensible choice. Many trail runners do it.

**Going down**

- Keep your steps short and quick, and land with your foot under you rather than far in front.
- Look a few metres ahead, not at your feet.
- Stay relaxed and let your arms move out for balance on rough ground.
- Slow down when the ground is loose, wet or steep. A fall costs far more than a few seconds.
