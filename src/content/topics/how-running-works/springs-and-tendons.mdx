## A leg is a spring

Watch a runner from the side and the body rises and falls with each step. It is lowest in the middle of the time the foot is on the ground, and highest in the air between steps. That is what a ball does when it bounces on a spring.

Scientists use exactly that picture, called the spring-mass model: the whole body is a single mass, bouncing on one springy leg<Cite id="H1" />. It leaves out almost everything, yet it captures how running works surprisingly well. The Spring lab above puts that model next to the 3D runner, so you can see them move together.

In each step:

1. **Landing.** The foot touches down a little ahead of the body. The leg spring starts to squash.
2. **First half of contact.** The body keeps moving forward and down. Hip, knee and ankle bend, and the spring is squashed most when the body passes over the foot.
3. **Second half.** The spring recoils, pushing the body up and forward.
4. **In the air.** The spring is unloaded. The leg swings forward to land again.

## Where the springs are

The "leg spring" is not one part. It is the sum of many tissues that stretch and recoil, and the muscles that hold them at the right tension.

- **The Achilles tendon** joins the calf muscles to the heel. As the ankle bends on landing, it is stretched, and it recoils in push-off.
- **The arch of the foot** flattens a little under load and springs back.

Both store energy on landing and return part of it<Cite id="R5" />. Tendon is a very good spring: tested in the lab, tendons give back most of the energy used to stretch them, and lose only a small share as heat<Cite id="H3" />. Muscles still do much of the work, but the springs mean they don't have to do all of it.

You can see the Achilles tendon light up as it stretches in the <TopicLink to="how-running-works/stride">stride lab</TopicLink>, and why humans have such long tendons in <TopicLink to="the-engine/why-humans-run-far">why humans can run far</TopicLink>.

## Stiff and soft springs

How much a spring squashes under a given force is its stiffness. Try the "Leg spring" slider in the lab:

- **Stiffer:** shorter ground contact, a higher peak force, and less drop of the body. Each bounce is quicker.
- **Softer:** longer contact, a lower peak, and more give.

The lab works these out from contact time, flight time, speed and body mass, with a well-tested shortcut that treats the force under the foot as a smooth curve<Cite id="H2" />. Notice that the average force over a whole step is always one body weight<Cite id="R2" />: a shorter contact simply squeezes the same push into less time.

Stiffness is not good or bad in itself. A review proposed that more stiffness helps performance, and that there may be a best range for avoiding injury, with some evidence linking very high stiffness with bone injuries and very low stiffness with soft-tissue injuries. That idea still needs studies that follow runners forward in time<Cite id="H4" />.

## Your body tunes the spring for you

You don't need to set your stiffness. Runners adjust it on their own: softer legs on hard ground and stiffer legs on soft ground, so the body bounces about the same on both<Cite id="S15" />.

What you can do is make the springs and the muscles around them stronger and more used to load, gradually. That is what <TopicLink to="strength-mobility-drills/strength-training">strength training</TopicLink> and gradual build-ups in <TopicLink to="training/building-up">training</TopicLink> do.
