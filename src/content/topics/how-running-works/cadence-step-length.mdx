## Two ways to go faster

Running speed is just steps per minute (cadence) multiplied by the length of each step. At 6:00 per km and 165 steps per minute, each step is about 1.01 m<Cite id="P2" />.

To speed up, you take more steps, longer steps, or both. Most runners do both. In the stride lab's model of a typical recreational runner, cadence climbs gently with speed while step length grows much more<Cite id="R1" />: drag its speed slider and watch the numbers.

## The 180 myth

You may have heard that everyone should run at 180 steps per minute. That number comes from coach Jack Daniels counting elite runners' cadence at the 1984 Olympics, not from a study of what suits each runner<Cite id="P1" />. Elite runners at race pace run fast, and cadence rises with speed. A recreational runner at an easy pace will usually be lower, and that's normal.

## Your body finds a good mix

Left to themselves, runners are good at choosing their stride. In 10 recreational runners on a treadmill, the stride length each chose freely was close to the one that used the least oxygen; much longer or shorter strides cost more<Cite id="H7" />.

So for most people, cadence is not something to fix. It is something to know.

## Why a small increase can help

There is one good reason to nudge cadence up: load. Raising step rate by 5 to 10% at the same speed reduced loading at the knee and hip<Cite id="R8" />. A systematic review found the same direction: a higher step rate went with less vertical bounce, lower ground forces and less energy absorbed at the knee<Cite id="H8" />.

Shorter, quicker steps tend to land the foot closer under the body, with less braking. This is why physiotherapists sometimes use a small cadence increase for runners with knee pain. It is a way to spread load differently, not a cure.

<Box kind="try" title="Find and nudge your cadence">

1. Count your steps for 30 seconds on an easy run and double it, or use the <TopicLink to="tools/cadence-counter">cadence counter</TopicLink>.
2. If you want to raise it, aim for about 5% more at the same easy pace. Use a metronome for a few minutes at a time.
3. Give it a few weeks. If it never feels natural, it's fine to let it go.

</Box>

## What else changes step length

- **Hills:** steps shorten uphill and lengthen downhill. See <TopicLink to="how-running-works/hills">uphill and downhill</TopicLink>.
- **Fatigue:** late in a long run, steps often shorten and contact gets longer.
- **Height and leg length:** legs differ from one runner to the next, and a single cadence number ignores that. One more reason not to chase it.
