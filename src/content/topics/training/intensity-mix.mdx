## Most of the running is easy

When researchers looked at how well-trained endurance athletes in several sports actually train, they found a similar pattern: about 80% of training sessions were at low intensity<Cite id="S9" />. The rest were harder.

This is often called the 80/20 pattern. It isn't a law, and the exact split varies, but it describes what most successful endurance athletes do. It also suits beginners and regular runners: easy running is what you can recover from quickly, so it's what lets you run often.

## What "easy" means

Easy is an effort where you can still talk in full sentences. In lab tests, the point where talking stops being comfortable sat close to the ventilatory threshold, a marker between moderate and hard exercise<Cite id="S7" />. Below it is easy.

That makes the <TopicLink to="start-running/talk-test">talk test</TopicLink> a free, reliable way to set your easy pace. You don't need a heart-rate monitor, although <TopicLink to="the-engine/heart-rate-zones">heart-rate zones</TopicLink> can help too.

Easy pace changes from day to day. On a hot day, a hilly route or after a bad night's sleep, the same effort is slower. Go by the effort, not the number on your watch.

## Three zones

Physiologists often split effort into three zones, using two thresholds as the boundaries<Cite id="E11" />:

| Zone | Talk test | What it feels like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Easy** | Full sentences | Relaxed. You could keep going for a long time. |
| **2. Steady** | Short phrases | Comfortably hard. Concentration needed. |
| **3. Hard** | A few words | Hard. Sustainable for minutes, not hours. |

The upper boundary is close to your <TopicLink to="the-engine/lactate-thresholds">critical speed</TopicLink>. Below it, your oxygen use and blood lactate settle to a steady level. Above it, they keep rising until you have to stop<Cite id="E13" />. The talk-test labels are a rough guide to these zones, not an exact match.

In the 80/20 pattern, most training is in zone 1, with a smaller share of harder work on top.

## What the studies show

A few trials have compared training mixes directly. They are small, so treat them as support for the pattern rather than proof.

- **Sub-elite runners.** In 12 runners trained for about 5 months, the group that did more of its running at low intensity improved a 10.4 km cross-country race by more, about 157 seconds against 121 seconds, than the group doing more moderately hard running<Cite id="T1" />.
- **Well-trained endurance athletes.** In 48 athletes over 9 weeks, a "polarised" programme, mostly easy with some hard work and little in between, raised peak oxygen uptake by about 12%. That was more than threshold-heavy, interval-heavy or high-volume programmes<Cite id="T2" />.

Easy running is not wasted time. Steady, continuous training raises VO2max a lot compared with no training, and intervals add only a possibly small extra gain on top<Cite id="E9" />. See <TopicLink to="the-engine/vo2max">VO2max</TopicLink>.

## The middle-zone trap

Many runners end up doing most of their runs at the same comfortably hard effort: a bit too quick to be easy, not hard enough to be a real workout. Coaches often call this the grey zone. It feels productive, but it can leave you too tired to run the hard sessions well, and it limits how much you can run.

The fix is simple, if not always easy on the ego:

- Make easy runs truly easy, even if that means a pace that feels slow, or walking up hills.
- Make your one or two harder sessions purposeful. See <TopicLink to="training/workouts">workouts explained</TopicLink>.
- If a run was meant to be easy and you couldn't talk, slow down next time.

<Box kind="try" title="Try this">

On your next easy run, try to say a full sentence out loud every few minutes, or run with a friend and chat. If you can't, slow down until you can. It may feel much slower than you're used to; that's fine.
