## Load, then recover

Training works by giving your body a bit more than it's used to, then giving it time to adapt. Hard work matched with enough recovery leads to improvement. When recovery keeps falling short, performance can stay down for weeks or longer<Cite id="F12" />. See <TopicLink to="fuel-recovery/rest-days">rest days</TopicLink>.

Coaches often point out that you can feel fit enough for more before your legs are ready for it, so it's easy to build up faster than your muscles, tendons and bones would like. Building gradually gives them time.

## New runners are most at risk

Pooling studies, novice runners had about 17.8 injuries per 1,000 hours of running, against about 7.7 for recreational runners<Cite id="S6" />. One likely reason is that a new runner's body hasn't yet adapted to the pounding of running. So the first year or two is when patience pays off most.

## The 10% rule, and big jumps

A common rule says to add no more than 10% to your weekly running each week. In a trial with 532 novice runners, a 13-week programme built on the 10% rule had about as many injuries as a standard 8-week programme: roughly one runner in five in each group<Cite id="S4" />.

Big jumps are a different matter. In 873 novice runners, those whose weekly distance rose by more than 30% had more of some overuse injuries than those whose distance rose by less than 10%<Cite id="S5" />. That's a link, not proof that the jump caused the injuries, but it fits with common sense.

## What to add first

Change one thing at a time. A common order is:

1. **More runs.** Go from three runs a week to four, keeping each one easy. Spreading running over more days keeps each run manageable.
2. **Longer runs.** Lengthen one run a week into a long run, by 5 or 10 minutes at a time.
3. **Faster running.** Only once you've been running regularly for a few months, add strides, then one workout a week. See <TopicLink to="training/workouts">workouts explained</TopicLink>.

Keep the extra running easy. See <TopicLink to="training/intensity-mix">the intensity mix</TopicLink>.

## Cutback weeks

Many plans build for two or three weeks, then hold steady or cut back for a week, at about two-thirds to three-quarters of the running, before building again. It gives your body a chance to catch up, and often you'll come out of it feeling fresher. This is coaching practice rather than a tested rule, but it's a simple way to avoid stacking up fatigue.

## Coming back after a break

After a break of a few weeks, from illness, injury or life, you lose fitness faster than you'd expect. In highly trained athletes, less than four weeks of stopped training brings a rapid fall in VO2max<Cite id="T5" />. Start back below where you left off, and build up again over a few weeks. If you stopped because of an injury, see <TopicLink to="injuries-health/coming-back">coming back after injury</TopicLink>.

## Signs you're doing too much

Ease back for a few days if you notice:

- Aches that are still there the next morning, or get worse from run to run.
- Easy runs feeling unusually hard, or your easy pace getting slower.
- Feeling tired all the time, sleeping badly or losing interest in running.

Strength training also helps your body cope with the load. See <TopicLink to="strength-mobility-drills/strength-training">strength training for runners</TopicLink>.
