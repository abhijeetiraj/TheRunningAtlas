## What it is

Cross-training means any exercise other than running that trains your aerobic fitness: your heart, lungs and blood vessels. Strength training is a separate thing, and runners benefit from both. See <TopicLink to="strength-mobility-drills/strength-training">strength training for runners</TopicLink>.

Any exercise that keeps you breathing hard for a while trains your heart and lungs. VO2max rises with steady training as well as with intervals<Cite id="E9" />. Training is also specific, though: you get best at what you practise. That's why cross-training adds to running rather than replacing it.

## The options

<div class="table-scroll">

| Activity | Impact | Closeness to running | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Deep-water running** | None | High | Running movements in deep water with a float belt |
| **Elliptical** | Low | High | Similar leg movement, no landing |
| **Cycling** | Low | Medium | Works the thighs hard; easy to do for long |
| **Rowing** | Low | Medium | Whole body, good for hard efforts |
| **Swimming** | None | Low | Mostly the upper body for the heart and lungs |
| **Brisk or uphill walking** | Low | Medium | Good for beginners and for easy days |

</div>

The "closeness" column is a rough guide to how much of the movement matches running, not a measured value.

## When to use it

- **To add fitness without more running.** If more running leaves you sore or injured, one or two cross-training sessions a week can add aerobic work with less load on your legs.
- **On easy or rest days.** A gentle bike ride or swim can feel good after a hard day. Keep it easy, so it doesn't eat into your recovery.
- **When injured.** This is where cross-training matters most. In highly trained athletes, less than four weeks of stopped or much reduced training brings a rapid fall in VO2max and blood volume<Cite id="T5" />. Cross-training slows that loss.

## Deep-water running

In deep water, with a float belt so your feet don't touch the bottom, you run with the same movements but with no landing at all. A review found that trained endurance athletes kept their aerobic performance with deep-water running for up to six weeks<Cite id="T6" />.

It feels different from land running. Your peak heart rate and oxygen use are lower in the water<Cite id="T6" />, so the same effort shows a lower heart rate. Go by how hard it feels, and keep a good upright running posture. Interval sessions in the pool can keep it interesting.

## What doesn't carry over

Cross-training keeps your heart and lungs fit, but your legs adapt to the specific load of running. The pounding of each landing, and the springy work of your tendons, only come from running itself. See <TopicLink to="how-running-works/springs-and-tendons">springs and tendons</TopicLink>.

So when you come back after a break filled with cross-training, your breathing may feel ready before your legs are. Build your running back up gradually, even if you feel fit. See <TopicLink to="training/building-up">building up gradually</TopicLink>.

<Box kind="care">

If you're cross-training because of an injury, ask a physiotherapist or doctor which activities are fine for it. Stop any activity that makes the pain worse. See <TopicLink to="injuries-health/coming-back">coming back after injury</TopicLink>.

</Box>
