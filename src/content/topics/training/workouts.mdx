## The menu

Here are the common types of run, with the effort for each by the <TopicLink to="start-running/talk-test">talk test</TopicLink>. The examples are typical coaching sessions, not tested prescriptions<Cite id="T9" />. Shorten them to suit where you are.

| Run | Effort | An example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Easy run** | Full sentences | 30 to 60 minutes, relaxed |
| **Recovery run** | Very easy, chatty | 20 to 40 minutes, the day after a hard session |
| **Long run** | Easy, full sentences | The longest run of the week, often 25 to 30% of it |
| **Strides** | Fast but relaxed | 4 to 6 × 20 seconds, walk back, after an easy run |
| **Tempo run** | Short phrases | 15 to 25 minutes steady, after a warm-up |
| **Threshold intervals** | Short phrases | 3 to 5 × 6 minutes, 1 to 2 minutes easy jog between |
| **VO2max intervals** | A few words | 4 to 6 × 3 minutes, equal time easy jog between |
| **Hill repeats** | Hard, a few words | 6 to 10 × 45 to 60 seconds uphill, walk or jog down |
| **Fartlek** | Mixed | Faster bursts to a lamp-post or a tree, as you feel |

Every workout starts with 10 to 15 minutes of easy running, and ends with a few minutes of easy running or walking. See the <TopicLink to="strength-mobility-drills/warm-up">warm-up studio</TopicLink>.

## Easy, recovery and long runs

These make up most of your running, and most of your fitness. Easy running trains your heart and blood vessels, and the small, slow-twitch muscle fibres that switch on first at easy efforts<Cite id="E20" />, and you recover from it quickly. Steady, continuous training raises VO2max a lot compared with no training<Cite id="E9" />. See <TopicLink to="training/intensity-mix">the intensity mix</TopicLink> and <TopicLink to="the-engine/muscle-fibres">muscle fibres</TopicLink>.

The long run is the same effort, for longer. It builds endurance and gets your body and mind used to time on your feet. For half and full marathons, it's also where you practise eating and drinking on the run. See <TopicLink to="fuel-recovery/before-during">before and during runs</TopicLink>.

## Tempo and threshold runs

There's an effort above which your body can no longer keep up: oxygen use and blood lactate keep climbing until you have to stop. Below it, they settle to a steady level. That boundary is called critical speed, and it sits close to what many runners call threshold pace<Cite id="E13" />. Different labs define thresholds in different ways, and they don't all measure the same thing<Cite id="E11" />.

A tempo run sits just below that boundary: hard enough to need concentration, steady enough to hold for 20 minutes or more. Breaking it into longer pieces with short jogs, as threshold intervals, lets you do a bit more at the same effort.

To estimate your own critical speed from two race or time-trial results, use the calculator on <TopicLink to="the-engine/lactate-thresholds">lactate thresholds</TopicLink>.

## Intervals

Intervals are faster than critical speed, so you can only hold them for a few minutes. Above that boundary, you have a fixed reserve of distance you can run before you're spent<Cite id="E12" />. Rests between pieces let you spend more total time at a hard effort than you could in one go.

Intervals raised VO2max a lot in trials, with a possibly small extra gain over continuous training<Cite id="E9" />. They're also the hardest sessions to recover from, so one a week is plenty for most runners. See <TopicLink to="the-engine/vo2max">VO2max</TopicLink>.

## Hill repeats

Short, hard efforts uphill work your heart and lungs much like intervals, with the extra push of lifting your body every step. Because you're going slower, there's usually less pounding than in fast running on the flat. Walk or jog back down. See <TopicLink to="how-running-works/hills">uphill and downhill</TopicLink>.

## Strides

Strides are short, fast, relaxed runs of about 15 to 25 seconds, building up to near your fastest smooth pace and easing off, with a full walk-back rest. They're not tiring; the aim is quick, relaxed form. They're a gentle way to bring some speed into your week, and a good first step before proper workouts.

## Fartlek

Fartlek means "speed play" in Swedish. In the middle of an easy run, you add faster bursts of any length, to the next corner, the top of a hill or for a song, then ease back until you feel ready for another. It's unstructured and fun, and a good way to start harder running without a watch.

## Putting it together

A simple week for a regular runner might look like this:

- Two or three easy runs, one with strides at the end.
- One workout: a tempo run, threshold intervals, intervals or hills.
- One long run.
- At least one rest day. See <TopicLink to="fuel-recovery/rest-days">rest days</TopicLink>.

Coaches usually keep hard days apart, with easy days or rest between. A long run counts as a demanding day too.

<Box kind="try" title="Your first workout">

After an easy run, add 4 to 6 strides on a flat stretch. Do that once or twice a week for a few weeks. Then swap one easy run for a short fartlek: 6 to 8 bursts of 30 to 60 seconds, with easy running until you've recovered.

</Box>

<Box kind="care">

Hard sessions should feel hard, not painful. If a workout gives you sharp pain, pain that changes how you run, or pain that's worse the next day, stop the workouts and get it checked. Chest pain, unusual breathlessness or feeling faint are reasons to stop running straight away and seek care now. See <TopicLink to="injuries-health/when-to-get-checked">when to stop and get checked</TopicLink>.

</Box>
