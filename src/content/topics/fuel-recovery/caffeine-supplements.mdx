<Box kind="care" title="Stop and seek care now">

**Stop and seek care now** if, after caffeine or a supplement, you have chest pain, a racing or irregular heartbeat that doesn't settle, feel faint, or are very shaky or anxious. **Call emergency services** if someone collapses, is confused or has a seizure.

Ask a doctor or pharmacist before using caffeine or any supplement if you're pregnant, have a heart condition, high blood pressure, anxiety or another health condition, or take regular medicines.

</Box>

## Caffeine

Caffeine has consistently improved exercise performance at doses of 3 to 6 mg per kg of body weight, most often taken about 60 minutes before. Very high doses, around 9 mg per kg, bring many side effects and don't seem to be needed<Cite id="F13" />.

People respond differently, so how it suits you is something to find out in training, not on race day.

## Safe limits

The European Food Safety Authority's review of caffeine safety concluded that, for healthy adults<Cite id="F14" />:

- Up to **400 mg a day**, from all sources, raises no safety concerns.
- Single doses up to **200 mg** raise no safety concerns.
- In **pregnancy**, the daily figure is **200 mg**.
- Single doses of **100 mg** may affect sleep in some adults if taken close to bedtime. See <TopicLink to="fuel-recovery/sleep">sleep</TopicLink>.

The performance range of 3 to 6 mg per kg can reach or pass these limits. For a 70 kg runner, 3 to 6 mg per kg is 210 to 420 mg. So for a race, start at the low end of the range or below it, count every source on the day (coffee, tea, cola, energy drinks, caffeinated gels and tablets), and keep the rest of the day's caffeine low.

We don't offer a caffeine dose calculator. How much is right depends on your health, your usual caffeine and how you react, and a number on a screen can't check those.

## Using caffeine well

- **Try it in training first**, before a long run or a workout.
- **Start small.** If you're new to caffeine, a lot less than the range above may be enough.
- **Read labels.** Caffeinated gels and energy drinks vary a lot.
- **Mind the timing.** Late-day caffeine can cost you sleep, which costs you recovery.

## Other supplements

An IOC consensus statement found that only a few supplements, including caffeine, creatine, some buffering agents and nitrate, have good evidence of improving performance<Cite id="F15" />.

The same statement warned that supplements can be contaminated with substances banned in sport, and that responses vary between people<Cite id="F15" />. If you're tested for banned substances in your sport, take this seriously.

## When it's a medical question

Some supplements, such as iron or vitamin D, are used to correct a deficiency. Whether you need one is a medical question: ask a doctor, who may suggest a blood test. Taking them without a reason isn't a shortcut to better running.
