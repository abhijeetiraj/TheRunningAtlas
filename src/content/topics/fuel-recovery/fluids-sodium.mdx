<Box kind="care" title="Low blood sodium: act now">

**Call emergency services** if a runner is confused, agitated, has a seizure or collapses during or after a long event. Low blood sodium (hyponatraemia), heat stroke and other causes can look alike, and only a blood test can tell them apart<Cite id="F18" />. Don't give them lots of water.

**Stop and seek care now** if, during or after a long run, you have a headache with vomiting, or you feel light-headed, dizzy, sick or bloated, especially if you've been drinking a lot or your rings or watch strap feel tight<Cite id="F18" />. Don't keep drinking water to "fix" it.

</Box>

## Drink to thirst

The international consensus on low blood sodium during exercise advises using thirst to guide how much to drink<Cite id="F7" />. For most runs under an hour, that means you don't need to carry anything. On longer runs, take drinks when you're thirsty, and know where you can get water.

Drinking as much as you can, or drinking well ahead of thirst, can lead you to drink far more than you sweat. That's the main cause of exercise-associated hyponatraemia<Cite id="E45" />.

## How much you sweat

Sweat rates and the salt in sweat vary a lot between people, so fluid plans should be individual. Weighing yourself before and after exercise estimates how much you lose. A common aim is to avoid losing more than about 2% of your body weight through water loss<Cite id="E30" />. For a 70 kg runner, that's 1.4 kg.

To measure your own sweat rate, weigh yourself in minimal clothing before and after a run of about an hour, and note what you drank. The calculator does the rest.

## Drinking too much

At the 2002 Boston Marathon, 13% of 488 runners who gave a blood sample at the finish had low blood sodium. Weight gain during the race, a sign of drinking more than was lost, was the strongest link<Cite id="E34" />.

Low blood sodium during or after exercise is mostly caused by drinking more than you lose. Severe cases can cause confusion, seizures and coma, and need emergency treatment<Cite id="E45" />.

Early on, it may cause no symptoms, or mild ones such as light-headedness, dizziness, nausea and puffiness<Cite id="F18" />. These are easy to mistake for dehydration, which is why drinking more is the wrong reflex.

The simplest check: **you shouldn't finish a run heavier than you started.** If you do, you drank more than you needed.

## Sodium

You lose sodium in sweat, and how much varies between people and with intensity, heat and heat acclimation. Sweat testing can guide sodium replacement only when it's done and interpreted properly<Cite id="F8" />.

For most runs, the sodium in your normal meals is enough. For long events, especially in the heat, many runners use sports drinks, salty snacks or electrolyte tablets. Taking sodium doesn't make it safe to overdrink: drinking more than you sweat is still the main risk<Cite id="E45" />.

## A long-run plan

- Drink to thirst, and have water available on long runs.
- Know your sweat rate in warm and cool weather.
- On long, hot days, sports drinks or salty foods can help; practise with them in training.
- Weigh yourself after a long run now and then. If you're heavier, drink less next time.

<Box kind="note" title="Heat makes it harder">

In hot weather you sweat more, and dehydration can make heat illness worse. Slow down, run in cooler parts of the day, and know the warning signs. See <TopicLink to="the-engine/heat-and-sweat">heat and sweat</TopicLink>.

</Box>
