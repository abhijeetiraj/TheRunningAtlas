## How much energy running uses

Running on the flat costs about 1 kcal for each kilogram of body weight for each kilometre, and this changes little with speed<Cite id="E28" />. So a 70 kg runner uses roughly 700 kcal over 10 km, on top of what they'd use anyway, whether they run it in 45 minutes or 75.

For most people running a few times a week, that's a modest extra. Ordinary meals cover it, with perhaps a bit more on long-run days. See <TopicLink to="the-engine/carbs-and-fat">carbs and fat as fuel</TopicLink>.

## Carbohydrate

Carbohydrate is the main fuel for harder running, and your muscles store it as glycogen. A diet high in carbohydrate raised muscle glycogen and let people exercise for longer than a diet low in it<Cite id="E27" />.

A joint position statement from sports nutrition bodies suggests daily carbohydrate in line with how much you train<Cite id="F1" />:

<div class="table-scroll">

| Training | Carbohydrate per kg of body weight a day | For a 60 kg runner | For a 75 kg runner |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Light | 3 to 5 g | 180 to 300 g | 225 to 375 g |
| Moderate, about an hour a day | 5 to 7 g | 300 to 420 g | 375 to 525 g |
| High, 1 to 3 hours a day | 6 to 10 g | 360 to 600 g | 450 to 750 g |
| Very heavy | 8 to 12 g | 480 to 720 g | 600 to 900 g |

</div>

You don't need to count. These numbers mainly show that carbohydrate needs grow with training: on heavy weeks, eat more bread, rice, pasta, potatoes, oats, fruit and similar foods, and less on light weeks.

## Protein

Protein repairs and builds muscle. The same statement suggests about 1.2 to 2.0 g per kg of body weight a day for athletes, spread over the day and after key sessions<Cite id="F2" />. For a 70 kg runner, that's about 85 to 140 g a day.

A protein food at each meal is a simple way to get there: meat, fish, eggs, dairy, beans, lentils, tofu and similar foods all count.

## A simple plate

A useful way to think about meals, rather than numbers:

- **A protein food** at every meal.
- **A carbohydrate food**, more of it on heavy training days.
- **Vegetables and fruit**, plenty and varied.
- **Some fats**, such as oils, nuts, seeds and dairy.
- **Water or other drinks** through the day. See <TopicLink to="fuel-recovery/fluids-sodium">fluids and sodium</TopicLink>.

Eat something with carbohydrate and protein in the hours after hard sessions and long runs. For eating around runs, see <TopicLink to="fuel-recovery/before-during">before and during runs</TopicLink>.

## Eating too little

When the energy you eat doesn't cover the energy you use in exercise and daily life, many body systems suffer, including bone health<Cite id="E43" />. The IOC calls this relative energy deficiency in sport. It includes, but isn't limited to, effects on metabolism, periods, bones, immunity, muscle building and heart health, and it affects men as well as women<Cite id="F16" />.

It can happen by accident, when training goes up and eating doesn't, or on purpose, when trying to lose weight while training hard. See <TopicLink to="different-runners/red-s">low energy availability (RED-S)</TopicLink>.

<Box kind="care">

Talk to a doctor if your periods have become irregular or stopped, if you've had a stress fracture, if you're losing weight without meaning to, or if you're often tired, ill or injured. Seek help too if worries about food, weight or your body are taking over. These aren't things to sort out by running more or eating less.

</Box>
