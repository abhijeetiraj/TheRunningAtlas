## Before

For an easy run of under an hour, you don't need anything special. Many people run comfortably on an empty stomach first thing; others like a small snack. Do what feels good.

For longer or harder runs, a carbohydrate meal beforehand tops up your stores. Before exercise lasting more than an hour, sports nutrition guidelines suggest 1 to 4 g of carbohydrate per kg of body weight, eaten 1 to 4 hours before<Cite id="F3" />. For a 65 kg runner, that's about 65 to 260 g.

Some common choices, which food labels can help you add up:

- A bowl of porridge with a banana and honey.
- Toast or a bagel with jam, honey or peanut butter, and a glass of juice.
- Rice or pasta with a light sauce, for an afternoon or evening run.

Leave more time after a bigger meal, and keep fat, fibre and spice lower before hard efforts if they upset your stomach. Eating, or drinking a lot (especially sugary drinks), shortly before running seems to make a stitch more likely<Cite id="F20" />.

## During

Your body's carbohydrate store is limited, and harder running uses it faster. On longer runs, carbohydrate taken on the way helps you keep going. See <TopicLink to="the-engine/carbs-and-fat">carbs and fat as fuel</TopicLink>.

Suggested amounts rise with the length of the effort<Cite id="F4" />:

| How long | Carbohydrate during |
| --- | --- |
| Under 1 hour | Little or none needed |
| 1 to 2 hours | Up to about 30 g an hour |
| 2 to 3 hours | Up to about 60 g an hour |
| Over about 2.5 hours | Up to about 90 g an hour |

Above about 60 g an hour, you need a mix of glucose and fructose<Cite id="F4" />. Many gels and drinks say on the label if they use a mix.

Use the planner to turn the guide into servings of the gels, chews or drink you use. It's arithmetic on the guide above<Cite id="F6" />.

## Gels, drinks or real food?

All of them work. What matters is how much carbohydrate you get, and what your stomach tolerates.

- **Gels** are compact and quick. The carbohydrate in each varies by brand, so check the label. Take them with a few sips of water.
- **Sports drinks** give fluid, carbohydrate and sodium together, but you need to drink quite a lot to get much carbohydrate.
- **Chews, bars and real food**, such as bananas, dried fruit or small sandwiches, suit slower, longer efforts and trail running.

## Train your gut

Your gut adapts to what it gets. A diet high in carbohydrate raises its capacity to absorb carbohydrate, and practising eating and drinking during training may reduce stomach problems, though few studies have tested this directly<Cite id="F5" />.

So use long runs as rehearsals:

- Start with a small amount, for example 20 to 30 g an hour, and build up over weeks.
- Use the same gels or drinks you'll use in the race. Find out what's offered on the course.
- Practise at race pace in some long runs too. Many runners find their stomach copes less well at faster speeds.

## After

After an easy run, your next normal meal is enough. After a long run or a hard session, have a meal or snack with carbohydrate and protein within a few hours. See <TopicLink to="fuel-recovery/everyday-eating">everyday eating</TopicLink>.

If you have less than 8 hours before another demanding session, refuel faster. The guide is about 1 to 1.2 g of carbohydrate per kg of body weight per hour for the first 4 hours, then back to normal eating<Cite id="F17" />.
