## How much

Sleep experts recommend that adults sleep 7 hours or more a night on a regular basis<Cite id="F9" />. Training adds to the load your body recovers from, so if you're running a lot, aim for the upper end of what works for you.

The number of hours isn't the only thing. Regular sleep and wake times, and sleep that isn't broken up, matter too.

## Sleep and injuries

In 112 school athletes, those who slept less than 8 hours a night on average had about 1.7 times the odds of injury<Cite id="F10" />. This was a small study looking back, so it's a link, not proof: athletes who sleep less may also differ in other ways, such as training more or being more stressed.

## Sleep and performance

In 11 college basketball players who extended their sleep for several weeks, sprint times and shooting accuracy improved<Cite id="F11" />. That's a small study in another sport, but it fits with what many athletes notice: they train and race better when they've slept well.

Many runners sleep poorly the night before a race, and nerves are normal. Try not to let one bad night worry you, and aim to sleep well through the week before.

## Sleeping better

These are common, simple habits rather than tested running advice:

- **Keep regular times**, including at weekends, as far as you can.
- **Watch the caffeine.** Single doses of 100 mg of caffeine may affect sleep in some adults if taken close to bedtime<Cite id="F19" />. If you sleep badly, keep coffee, tea, cola and caffeinated gels to the morning and early afternoon.
- **Wind down.** Dim lights and put screens away for a while before bed.
- **Dark, quiet, cool room.**
- **Time hard sessions.** If late hard runs leave you wired, try them earlier in the day.
- **Nap if you need to.** Some runners nap on heavy training days. Keep it short, so it doesn't stop you sleeping at night.

If your training is going up and your sleep is going down, that's a reason to ease back for a few days. See <TopicLink to="fuel-recovery/rest-days">rest days</TopicLink>.

<Box kind="care">

Talk to a doctor if you often sleep badly for weeks, if a partner notices you stop breathing or snore loudly in your sleep, or if you feel very sleepy during the day despite enough time in bed. These can have causes that need treatment.
