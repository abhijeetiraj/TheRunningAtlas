## Three days a week to start

Three sessions a week, with a rest day between each, is the pattern most beginner plans use. Tuesday, Thursday and Saturday works for many people. The rest days are not wasted: they are when your body repairs and adapts to the work you did.

Three run-walk sessions of about half an hour, plus some brisk walking on other days, gets you close to what the World Health Organization recommends for adults: 150 to 300 minutes of moderate activity, or 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous activity, each week<Cite id="S3" />. For most people, running counts as vigorous and brisk walking as moderate.

## How far: count minutes first

In the first months, track time rather than distance. A 30-minute easy run is the same effort whether it covers 3 km or 5 km, and time doesn't push you to speed up to "get the kilometres in".

The <TopicLink to="start-running/first-run">nine-week run-walk plan</TopicLink> works this way: every session is set in minutes, and it takes you from your first run to 30 minutes of running.

## After the first nine weeks

Once you can run for 30 minutes, you have choices about what to add. Change one thing at a time:

- **A fourth day.** Add a short, easy run before making any run longer.
- **A longer run.** Let one run a week grow by five or ten minutes every week or two.
- **Some faster running.** Wait until running feels routine. <TopicLink to="training/workouts">Workouts explained</TopicLink> covers the options.

Keep most of your running at an easy, conversational effort. <TopicLink to="start-running/talk-test">The talk test</TopicLink> is the simplest way to check.

Every three or four weeks, have a lighter week with a bit less running. It gives your body a chance to catch up, and you often feel stronger the week after.

## The 10% rule, and what the evidence shows

You will hear that you shouldn't add more than 10% each week. It's a reasonable rough guide, but it isn't a tested safety limit.

In a trial with 532 novice runners training for a 4-mile race, a slower 13-week programme built on the 10% rule had about as many injuries as a standard 8-week programme: roughly one runner in five in each group<Cite id="S4" />. In another study of 873 novice runners, those whose weekly distance rose by more than 30% had more of some overuse injuries than those whose distance rose by less than 10%<Cite id="S5" />. That is a link, and it doesn't prove the jump caused the injuries.

The sensible reading is that there is no magic percentage, but big jumps are best avoided, especially when you are new or coming back after a break. New runners pick up injuries more often than experienced runners. Pooling studies, novices had about 17.8 injuries per 1,000 hours of running, against about 7.7 for recreational runners<Cite id="S6" />. Your tendons and bones take longer to adapt than your heart and lungs.

## Signs you're doing too much

Ease back for a few days, or take an extra rest day, if you notice:

- legs that feel heavy and stay heavy for days;
- runs that feel much harder than they should at the same easy pace;
- feeling tired all the time, sleeping badly, or dreading runs you used to enjoy.

<Box kind="care">

Stop running and get it checked if you have pain that makes you limp, pain that gets worse as you run, swelling, or pain in a bone that hurts to press. Running through it can turn a small problem into a long one.
