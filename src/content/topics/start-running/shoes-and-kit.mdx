## Shoes: comfort first

For years, running shoes were sold by foot type: "stability" shoes for feet that roll inwards (pronate), extra cushioning for high arches. The evidence for that has not held up.

- In 927 novice runners all given the same neutral shoe and followed for a year, runners whose feet pronated did not have more injuries than runners with neutral feet<Cite id="S11" />.
- In trials with army, air force and marine recruits, assigning shoes by the height of the foot arch did not reduce injuries<Cite id="S12" />.
- Reviewing the research, a group of biomechanists argued that the best available guide is comfort: try several shoes, and pick the one that feels most comfortable<Cite id="S10" />.

So the practical advice is simple.

1. **Try on several pairs,** ideally at a running shop where you can jog in them.
2. **Pick the most comfortable,** not the most expensive, the most cushioned or the best-looking.
3. **Leave a thumb's width** between your longest toe and the end of the shoe. Feet swell a little on a run, so try shoes later in the day.
4. **Wear them on a few short runs** before a long one.

If a shoe gives you blisters, rubs or pinches, it's the wrong shoe for you, whatever it's meant to do. If you have had injuries before and a physiotherapist has advised a particular shoe or insert, follow their advice.

### When to replace them

You will often hear "every 500 to 800 km". That's a rule of thumb, not a tested limit. Replace shoes when the sole is worn through in places, the foam feels flat or uneven, or they stop feeling comfortable. Keeping a note of when you bought them helps.

For how foams, drop and carbon plates work, see <TopicLink to="how-running-works/shoe-science">shoe science</TopicLink>.

## Clothes

You don't need specialist clothing to start, but a few things make running more comfortable.

- **Fabrics that dry quickly.** Synthetic or merino tops and socks stay lighter and rub less than cotton once you sweat.
- **A supportive sports bra,** if you wear one. A good fit makes a big difference to comfort.
- **Layers in the cold,** so you can take one off as you warm up.
- **Anti-chafe balm** on longer runs, wherever things rub: inner thighs, underarms, under a bra strap, nipples.

## Safety kit

- **Your phone,** and a note of who to call in an emergency. Many phones can show emergency contacts on the lock screen.
- **Be seen in the dark:** bright colours with reflective strips, and a light where paths are unlit.
- **Headphones:** keep the volume low, or use one ear or open-ear headphones, so you can hear traffic, bikes and people.
- **Tell someone** your route if you run alone somewhere remote.

## Nice to have, not needed

A running watch, a heart-rate strap, a hydration vest and recovery gadgets can all be useful later. None of them is needed to start, and none of them replaces running easily, building up gradually and resting well.
