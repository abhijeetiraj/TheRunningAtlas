<Box kind="care" title="Stop and seek care now">

Stop running and seek care straight away if you have any one of these:

- pain, pressure or tightness in your chest, or pain spreading to your arm, neck or jaw;
- breathlessness that doesn't settle within a few minutes of stopping;
- feeling faint or dizzy, or a racing or irregular heartbeat that doesn't settle;
- a stitch-like pain that stays after you stop, or comes with fever, vomiting or a swollen, tender belly.

Call emergency services for chest pain, collapse, confusion or severe breathlessness.

</Box>

## What a stitch is

A stitch is a sharp or cramping pain in the side of the belly, usually just below the ribs, and sometimes in the tip of the shoulder. Its formal name is exercise-related transient abdominal pain. It's common in activities where the torso moves up and down over and over, like running and horse riding, and nobody knows for certain what causes it<Cite id="S16" />.

## What may help

The evidence on fixes is thin, so treat these as things to try rather than cures.

- **Slow down or walk** until it eases. It usually fades within a few minutes.
- **Breathe deeply** and let each breath out slowly.
- **Press firmly** on the painful spot with your fingers, and lean forward a little as you walk.
- **Leave a gap after eating.** Eating, or drinking a lot (especially sugary drinks), shortly before running seems to make a stitch more likely<Cite id="S17" />. Try leaving a couple of hours after a meal, and sip rather than gulp.

Stitches tend to come less often as you get fitter.

## Breathless when you start

Feeling out of breath is normal when you start running. Your heart and lungs are being asked for more than usual, and it's often a sign you're going a little too fast. The fix is to slow down:

- run slowly enough to talk in full sentences (<TopicLink to="start-running/talk-test">the talk test</TopicLink>);
- take walking breaks, as in <TopicLink to="start-running/first-run">run-walk</TopicLink>;
- start each run with five minutes of brisk walking.

Within a few weeks, the same pace feels noticeably easier.

## Breathing in rhythm

Some runners like to breathe in time with their steps, for example in for three steps and out for three. There's no strong evidence that it prevents stitches or makes you faster, but some people find it calming and it can help you settle into an easy effort. Try it if you're curious.

## Breathing problems to get checked

Make an appointment with your doctor if you often wheeze, cough or get a tight chest during or after running, or if breathlessness is getting worse over weeks rather than better. Exercise can trigger asthma, and it can usually be treated so you can keep running. <TopicLink to="different-runners/asthma-diabetes">Running with asthma or diabetes</TopicLink> has more.
