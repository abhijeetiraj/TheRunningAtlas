## Why start with run-walk

Running is a string of small jumps. Each step, you push off, fly for a moment and land again, and your legs catch your whole body weight and more. <TopicLink to="how-running-works/stride">The running stride lab</TopicLink> shows it in slow motion.

Your heart and lungs get used to this quickly, within a few weeks. Your muscles, tendons and bones adapt too, but more slowly. Walking breaks let them catch up. They also make the first session short and doable, which matters more than anything for coming back for the second.

That is why most plans for new runners start this way. The NHS's Couch to 5K, for example, begins with short runs between walks and builds to about 30 minutes of running over nine weeks, with three runs a week<Cite id="S1" />.

## Your first session

You need comfortable shoes, clothes you can move in, and about 35 minutes.

1. **Walk briskly for 5 minutes.** This is your warm-up. Your heart rate rises and your legs loosen.
2. **Run very easily for 1 minute.** Easy means you could talk in full sentences while you run. If you are gasping, slow down. A slow shuffle still counts as running.
3. **Walk for 2 minutes.** Let your breathing settle.
4. **Repeat** the run and the walk until you have run 8 times.
5. **Walk for 5 minutes** to cool down.

That's it. Don't worry about pace or distance in the first weeks: time is all you need to track.

## When to move on to the next week

Move on when the week you're on felt comfortable. That means you finished every session, you could talk while running, and you felt fine the next day.

It is normal to repeat weeks, or to drop back one. Heat, a bad night's sleep, a busy week or a cold can all make the same session feel harder. The plan works at your pace, not the calendar's.

Feeling a little stiff or sore in your legs a day or two after the first sessions is common. Pain that makes you limp, or that gets worse as you run, is different. <TopicLink to="start-running/soreness">Soreness: what is normal</TopicLink> explains the difference.

## Habits that help

- **Start from your door,** or from somewhere you go anyway. The less there is to organise, the more often you'll go.
- **Pick a flat, familiar loop** for the first weeks, so you can think about how you feel and not where you are.
- **Run at a time you can keep.** Same days, same time, becomes routine. <TopicLink to="start-running/staying-motivated">Staying motivated</TopicLink> has more on making it stick.
- **Breathe however feels natural.** There is no correct way. If you can't talk, you're going too fast.
- **Finish wanting more.** Ending a session feeling you could have done a little extra is a good sign, not a wasted run.

## Why it's worth it

Running doesn't have to be a lot to count. In a pooled analysis of 14 studies with more than 230,000 people, any amount of running was linked with a lower risk of dying from any cause during the studies' follow-up than no running at all, including less than 50 minutes a week<Cite id="S2" />. That is a link, not proof that running caused it, but it's an encouraging one.
