## Plan when and where

Wanting to run is not the same as running. What helps most is deciding, in advance, exactly when and where you will do it. Psychologists call these implementation intentions, or "if-then" plans: *if it's Tuesday at 7 am, then I put on my shoes and run my 30-minute loop.*

Across 94 studies, plans like this had a medium-to-large effect on whether people reached their goals<Cite id="S23" />. They work because they take the decision out of the moment: when Tuesday at 7 comes, there's nothing left to decide.

## Give it time

Habits take a while. In a study of 96 volunteers each building one new daily habit, it took a median of 66 days for the habit to feel automatic, and anywhere from 18 to 254 days depending on the person and the habit<Cite id="S22" />. Missing a day now and then didn't stop the habit forming.

So expect the first two months to take some effort. After that, it usually gets easier to get out of the door.

## Make starting easy

- **Lay out your kit** the night before, or run straight from work in clothes you packed.
- **Shrink the first step.** Promise yourself just ten minutes. Once you're out, you'll usually do the rest.
- **Run from your door,** on a route you already know.

## Track what matters

In the first months, track sessions, not speed. A simple tick on a calendar for every run done is a powerful thing to look at. Pace will improve by itself, and comparing it every run mostly makes easy runs too fast.

## Find your people

Running with others makes it harder to skip and easier to enjoy.

- **A friend** who runs at your pace, or a little slower.
- **A beginners' group** at a local running club. Many run courses for new runners.
- **parkrun,** a free, weekly, timed 5 km event held in parks in many countries<Cite id="S27" />. You can walk, jog or run it, and nobody finishes last: a volunteer walks at the back.

## Goals that help

A target gives your running a point. Good first goals are about doing, not speed:

- finish the nine-week <TopicLink to="start-running/first-run">run-walk plan</TopicLink>;
- run for 30 minutes without stopping;
- enter a 5 km event a few weeks after you can run 30 minutes.

## When you miss a run

You will miss runs: illness, work, family, weather. When it happens, just do the next planned session. Don't try to make up the missed one by running two days in a row or doubling a run.

If you've missed a week or two, drop back a week in your plan. After a longer break, drop back further. It comes back faster the second time.

Write down why you started running, in one sentence, and keep it where you'll see it: on your phone's lock screen or next to your shoes. On low days, it helps to remember.
