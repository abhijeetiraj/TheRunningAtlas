## How it works

In 1981, Peter Riegel fitted a simple power law to world-record times at distances lasting from about 3.5 minutes to nearly 4 hours<Cite id="P3" />:

<p class="formula"><span>new time</span><span>=</span><span>known time × (new distance ÷ known distance)<sup>1.06</sup></span></p>

The power of 1.06 means each doubling of distance slows your average pace by about 4%. The estimator applies it to the race you enter, then works out the pace for each distance<Cite id="P5" />. For example, a 52:30 10K gives about 1:56 for a half marathon and about 4:02 for a marathon.

## Where it goes wrong

The formula came from world records, set by runners trained for each distance. In a survey of 2,303 recreational runners, it predicted times well up to the half marathon, but its marathon predictions were too fast: by at least 10 minutes for half of the runners<Cite id="P4" />.

So:

- **Treat a marathon estimate from a shorter race as a best case**, especially for a first marathon. See <TopicLink to="racing/pacing">pacing</TopicLink>.
- **Use a recent, all-out race** on a similar course. A hilly or hot race will make the estimates slower.
- **Remember it assumes you train for the new distance.** A good 10K doesn't give you a marathon without the long runs. See <TopicLink to="training/plans">plans from 5K to marathon</TopicLink>.
