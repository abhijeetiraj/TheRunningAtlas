## Why runners should lift

Running is thousands of one-leg hops in a row. Each landing loads your calves, thighs and hips with two to three times your body weight<Cite id="H9" />. Strength training makes those muscles, and the tendons that join them to bone, better able to take that load.

Two kinds of benefit have been studied:

- **Running economy.** A review of 24 studies in middle- and long-distance runners found that adding strength training generally improved running economy by about 2 to 8% compared with running alone, and tended to improve time trials from 1.5 to 10 km. VO2max and body composition were usually unchanged, so it didn't make runners heavier<Cite id="M1" />. A separate pooled analysis came to a similar view on economy<Cite id="H15" />. See <TopicLink to="how-running-works/running-economy">running economy</TopicLink>.
- **Injuries.** Pooling 25 trials of over 26,000 people across many sports, strength training programmes were linked with the biggest drop in injuries; stretching programmes showed no clear protective effect<Cite id="W5" />. These were not all runners, so the size of the effect for running is less certain.

## How much, and how often

The American College of Sports Medicine advises adults to train each major muscle group on 2 to 3 days a week<Cite id="M2" />. For runners, the review above suggested 2 to 3 sessions a week<Cite id="M1" />.

A simple start:

- **Two sessions a week**, of about 20 to 30 minutes.
- **Four to six exercises** from the library, for example a squat or split squat, a calf raise, a single-leg deadlift and a hip exercise.
- **Two or three sets of each**, with a minute or so of rest. Choose a number of repetitions where the last two or three feel hard but your form holds.

The doses in the lab are common starting points, not tested prescriptions<Cite id="M5" />.

## Making it harder over time

Your body adapts to what you ask of it, so the work has to grow slowly:

1. **More repetitions or sets**, up to the top of the range.
2. **One leg instead of two**, for example a split squat instead of a squat, or single-leg calf raises.
3. **Slower lowering**, for example three seconds down.
4. **Added weight**: a backpack, dumbbells or a kettlebell, and later a gym.

Change one thing at a time, and expect some muscle soreness for a day or two after new exercises. That's normal and fades as you repeat them. See <TopicLink to="start-running/soreness">soreness</TopicLink>.

## Fitting it around running

- Put strength work on easy days or after an easy run, so your hard running days stay fresh.
- Avoid heavy leg work the day before a hard session or a race.
- In the last week or two before a big race, keep doing it, but lighter.

<Box kind="note" title="Running drills and plyometrics">

Hopping, bounding and skipping drills train the spring of your legs. They're more demanding than the library's exercises, so build a base of strength first. See <TopicLink to="strength-mobility-drills/drills">running drills</TopicLink>.

</Box>

<Box kind="care">

Strength work should feel like hard effort, not pain. Stop an exercise that causes sharp pain, pain in a joint, or pain that lasts into the next day, and have it checked. If you have a current injury, a physiotherapist can choose exercises that suit it.

</Box>
