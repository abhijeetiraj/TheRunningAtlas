## What it does

Pooling 21 studies, foam rolling before exercise slightly improved sprint performance, by about 0.7%, and flexibility, by about 4%. After exercise, it slightly reduced muscle pain, by about 6%, and the drop in sprint performance. The authors judged these effects small<Cite id="M4" />.

So foam rolling isn't magic, but it isn't useless either. For a runner, the most useful effect is probably that it can make tired, sore legs feel a bit better.

Why it works is still debated. You'll see claims that rolling "breaks up knots" or "releases fascia", the connective tissue around muscles. Treat those as ideas rather than established facts.

## When to roll

- **Before a run**, a minute or two of rolling on tight areas can be part of your warm-up. Follow it with moving warm-ups. See the <TopicLink to="strength-mobility-drills/warm-up">warm-up studio</TopicLink>.
- **After a run, or in the evening**, it's a way to ease sore legs. See <TopicLink to="start-running/soreness">soreness</TopicLink>.

## How to roll

| Area | How |
| --- | --- |
| **Calves** | Sit with the roller under one calf, hands behind you, and lift your hips. Roll from above the ankle to below the knee. Cross the other leg on top for more pressure. |
| **Front of thigh** | Lie face down, roller under the thighs, on your forearms. Roll from above the knee to the hip. |
| **Back of thigh** | Sit with the roller under your thighs and lift your hips. Roll from above the knee to below the seat. |
| **Seat** | Sit on the roller, cross one ankle over the other knee, and lean towards that side. |
| **Outer hip** | Lie on your side with the roller under the hip, just below the bone, and roll a short way. |

- Roll slowly, about 30 to 60 seconds per area. Pause on tender spots for a few breaths.
- Aim for a pressure that's uncomfortable but not painful. If you're tensing up or holding your breath, ease off.
- A firmer roller or a ball gives more pressure. A softer one is fine to start.

These are common practice rather than tested doses.

## What to avoid

- Bones and joints, such as your knee, the bony outer hip and your spine.
- A fresh injury, a bruise or a swollen area.
- Rolling hard on a pain that's there all the time; get it checked instead.

<Box kind="note" title="Rolling, stretching or strength?">

All three can make you more flexible. In a large review, strength training programmes were linked with the biggest drop in injuries, and stretching programmes showed no clear effect<Cite id="W5" />. Foam rolling wasn't part of it. If time is short, a strength session is probably the better use of it. See <TopicLink to="strength-mobility-drills/strength-training">strength training for runners</TopicLink>.

</Box>

<Box kind="care">

Ask a doctor before foam rolling if you have a condition that affects your blood vessels or blood clotting, or take blood-thinning medicine. Don't roll a calf that is painful, swollen, warm or red: stop and seek care now, as it needs checking the same day.

</Box>
