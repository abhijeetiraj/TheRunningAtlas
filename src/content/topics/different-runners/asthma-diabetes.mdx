<Box kind="care" title="Asthma attack or a severe low">

**In an asthma attack**, if you use a blue reliever inhaler, NHS advice is to sit up, take 1 puff every 30 to 60 seconds up to 10 puffs, and **call emergency services** (999 in the UK) if it isn't helping, you feel worse, or you don't have your inhaler. If you use a combination inhaler as your reliever (SMART or MART), follow your own action plan, as the instructions are different<Cite id="D17" />.

**For low blood sugar**, if a person is confused, can't safely swallow, has a seizure or is unconscious, **call emergency services**. Don't give food or drink to someone who can't swallow safely.

</Box>

This page is general information. Your own asthma or diabetes plan, from your doctor, nurse or diabetes team, comes first.

## Asthma and running

Exercise can narrow the airways in some people, which is called exercise-induced bronchoconstriction. For it, the American Thoracic Society strongly recommends<Cite id="D16" />:

- **An inhaled short-acting reliever before exercise**, typically about 15 minutes before.
- **A warm-up before planned exercise**, such as intervals or a mix of easy and harder efforts.

Some practical points:

- **Always carry your reliever** on runs, and tell running partners where it is.
- **Notice your triggers.** If certain conditions set you off, plan for them, and talk to your doctor or asthma nurse.
- **See your doctor or asthma nurse** if you need your reliever more often than usual, or if symptoms are stopping you from running. Your treatment may need a review.

## Diabetes and running

The American Diabetes Association advises checking glucose before and during exercise to prevent lows in people at risk of them, and an individual plan, because the challenges differ with diabetes type, activity and complications<Cite id="D19" />.

**Type 1 diabetes.** An international consensus suggests a starting blood glucose of about 7 to 10 mmol/L (126 to 180 mg/dL) for most adults before aerobic exercise of up to an hour. It uses insulin reductions and extra carbohydrate to lower the risk of a low<Cite id="D18" />. How much to change depends on you, so work it out with your diabetes team.

**Type 2 diabetes.** If you take insulin or other medicines that can cause lows, ask your diabetes team how to plan around runs. If you're not sure whether yours can, ask when you start.

Some practical points:

- **Carry fast-acting carbohydrate**, such as glucose tablets or a gel, on every run.
- **Know your own signs of a low**, and stop and treat it straight away.
- **Tell someone** your route, or run with others, and carry ID that says you have diabetes.
- **Ask your diabetes team** about lows later in the day or overnight after longer or harder runs.
