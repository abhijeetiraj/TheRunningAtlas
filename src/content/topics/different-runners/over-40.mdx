**See a doctor before you start or step up your training** if you have pain or discomfort in the chest, neck, jaw or arms; breathlessness at rest or with mild effort; dizziness or fainting; ankle swelling; palpitations; calf pain on walking; a known heart murmur; or unusual tiredness<Cite id="D11" />. ## How performance changes with age

In masters athletes, peak endurance running performance is kept until about age 35. It falls modestly until 50 to 60, and then falls more steeply<Cite id="D9" />.

The main reason is a falling VO2max, the most oxygen your body can use. Running economy, how much oxygen you need at a given pace, did not change with age in endurance-trained adults<Cite id="D9" />. See <TopicLink to="the-engine/vo2max">VO2max</TopicLink>.

## Staying active makes a difference

In 810 healthy adults aged 21 to 87, peak oxygen uptake fell faster with each decade: about 3 to 6% per 10 years in the 20s and 30s, and more than 20% per 10 years from the 70s. More active people were fitter at every age<Cite id="D10" />.

So age changes the numbers, and staying active still counts. If you're new to running, start where you are. See <TopicLink to="start-running/first-run">your first run</TopicLink>.

## Strength and bone

Strength training is worth adding. In runners, adding it generally improved running economy, by about 2 to 8% against running alone<Cite id="M1" />. See <TopicLink to="strength-mobility-drills/strength-training">strength training</TopicLink>.

Running is an impact exercise, so it loads bone. A UK consensus on osteoporosis recommends resistance and impact exercise to maximise bone strength, and advises against restricting activity based on bone density alone<Cite id="D6" />. If you've been told you have low bone density, ask your doctor or physiotherapist how to adapt your exercise.

## Training as you get older

There are no fixed rules for training as you get older. Go by how you respond:

- **Allow time to recover** between hard sessions, and add more if you feel flat or sore for longer.
- **Build up gradually**, especially when coming back after a break. See <TopicLink to="training/building-up">building up gradually</TopicLink>.
- **Keep some faster running** in the mix if you enjoy it and it suits your health. See <TopicLink to="training/intensity-mix">easy running and the intensity mix</TopicLink>.

When a runner's heart stops in a race, what bystanders do makes a big difference: bystander CPR was the strongest predictor of survival in a study of US marathons and half marathons<Cite id="I16" />. Learning CPR is worthwhile at any age.
