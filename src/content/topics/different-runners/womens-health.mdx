<Box kind="care" title="In pregnancy: stop and seek care now">

If you're pregnant, **stop exercising and seek care now** if you have any one of these: vaginal bleeding; regular, painful contractions; fluid leaking from the vagina; breathlessness before you start; dizziness; a headache; chest pain; muscle weakness that affects your balance; or calf pain or swelling<Cite id="D3" />.

**Call emergency services** for collapse, a seizure, or chest pain that doesn't settle.

</Box>

## Your cycle

Pooling 78 studies, performance might be trivially lower around your period (the early follicular phase) than at other times of the cycle. The evidence was mostly low quality and varied a lot between studies, so the authors advised a personal approach based on how each woman responds<Cite id="D1" />.

In practice, that means there's no need to plan your training around your cycle unless you notice a pattern. Some women keep a note of how runs feel alongside their cycle for a few months, and adjust from there.

If your periods become irregular or stop, see a doctor. Eating too little for the energy you use can affect periods and bone health<Cite id="D8" />. See <TopicLink to="different-runners/red-s">low energy availability</TopicLink>.

## Pregnancy

If you ran regularly before pregnancy, and you have no medical or pregnancy complications, you can generally keep running during pregnancy and after birth, with changes as your body changes. Routine restriction of activity is discouraged<Cite id="D2" />.

A Canadian guideline advises pregnant women without contraindications to build up to at least 150 minutes of moderate activity a week. The evidence it reviewed showed moderate benefits and no harms<Cite id="D4" />.

Talk to your midwife or doctor early on about running, and again if anything changes. You may find you want to slow down, run shorter or swap some runs for walking or swimming as pregnancy goes on. Go by how you feel, and keep to an easy effort you could talk at. See <TopicLink to="start-running/talk-test">the talk test</TopicLink>.

## After having a baby

UK physiotherapists' guidance suggests about 3 months after birth as the earliest time to consider running, after passing a set of strength and load tests and, ideally, an assessment by a pelvic health physiotherapist. It's based on limited evidence and clinical experience, and is meant as a guide, not a fixed protocol<Cite id="D5" />.

Ask your midwife, doctor or a pelvic health physiotherapist what to do before then. They can also help if you have leaking, a feeling of heaviness, or pain. When you start, restart with short run and walk sessions. See <TopicLink to="injuries-health/coming-back">coming back after injury</TopicLink> for how to build up.

## Bone health and menopause

Running is an impact exercise, so it loads your bones. A UK consensus statement on osteoporosis recommends resistance and impact exercise, such as running and jumping, to maximise bone strength, with adjustments for people who have had spinal fractures. It advises against restricting activity based on bone density alone<Cite id="D6" />.

So if you've been told you have low bone density, there's no need to stop running on that basis, but do ask your doctor or physiotherapist how to adapt your exercise. See <TopicLink to="strength-mobility-drills/strength-training">strength training</TopicLink> for resistance exercises.
