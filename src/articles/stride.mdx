## You run on springs

Walking works like a pendulum: you vault over a fairly stiff leg, and one foot is always on the ground. Running works more like a pogo stick. Each landing compresses the leg, the tendons stretch, and they spring back as you push off.

The Achilles tendon and the arch of the foot can store part of the energy of each landing and give it back<Cite id="R5" />, so your muscles do less of the work. Watch the green tendon in the lab: it stretches through every landing and recoils at push-off.

## Why faster running means bigger forces

Over a whole step, the ground has to push up on you with an average of exactly one body weight. Less, and you would sink; more, and you would fly away. That push can only happen while a foot is down<Cite id="R2" />.

As you speed up, each contact gets shorter, so the same push is packed into less time and the peak has to rise. For a smooth force curve:

<p class="formula"><span>peak force</span><span>≈</span><span>π/2 × (step time ÷ contact time) × body weight</span></p>

Real force curves are not perfectly smooth, so treat this as a good estimate, not a measurement.

## Heel, midfoot or forefoot?

Switch the foot strike in the lab and watch the force chart. Heel landings usually show a sharp impact peak in the first few hundredths of a second; forefoot landings usually do not<Cite id="R3" />.

That does not make one style safer. In a study of 19 female forefoot strikers and 19 matched heel strikers, the forefoot group had lower knee loading but higher Achilles tendon force<Cite id="R6" />. A review of 53 studies could not tell whether strike pattern changes injury risk, and switching to a forefoot strike made running less economical in the short term<Cite id="R7" />. Its authors did not recommend changing strike pattern for uninjured heel strikers.

If you do change how you land, change it gradually: the load moves towards your calves and Achilles tendon.

## Cadence and step length

Your speed is your cadence (steps per minute) times your step length. Move the speed slider: in this model, both rise as you speed up.

In one lab study, raising step rate by 5 to 10% at the same speed reduced loading at the knee and hip<Cite id="R8" />. Cadence also depends on your height, leg length and speed, so there is no single right number.
